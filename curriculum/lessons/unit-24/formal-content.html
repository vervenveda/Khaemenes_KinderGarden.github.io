Science, Access & Safety Commitments

Force is an interaction: a push or pull can change an object's motion. Do not teach force as a visible substance stored inside a moving toy. Motion evidence includes starting, stopping, speeding up, slowing down, or changing direction.
Transportation is broader than cars: walking/rolling, mobility devices, bicycles, buses, trains, boats, ferries, aircraft, delivery systems, and other modes may serve different purposes. No learner must disclose car ownership, disability, commute, home address, bus stop, or travel history.
Toy-scale only: no child-riding ramps, chairs/carts as vehicles, projectiles, roadway/parking-lot tests, traffic observation trips, steep furniture ramps, heavy rolling objects, or tests aimed at people/animals. Use lightweight teacher-approved objects and floor/tabletop models.

Monday · Force & Motion Detectives

A push or pull can change motion—but movement itself is evidence, not a magical property

Children connect an observed interaction to a specific change in motion.

Push / pull evidence

Use safe toy/model examples to identify a push or pull and the resulting change: start, stop, speed up, slow down, or change direction. A force is an interaction, not a substance that a toy “contains.”

Gravity and support

On a ramp, gravity contributes to motion downhill while the ramp surface supports and guides the object. Do not teach that the ramp “gives the toy force.”

Interaction → observation → explanation

  1. Observe a stationary toy before interaction.
  2. Apply or view one controlled push/pull.
  3. Name what changed in motion.
  4. Repeat with a different direction/strength only when safe.
  5. Distinguish the observed change from a guess about why.

Same push ≠ guaranteed same result

Object mass/shape, wheels, surface, slope, release, alignment, and friction can affect outcomes. Kindergarten reasoning should use evidence and conditions rather than “a stronger push always makes it go exactly twice as far.”

Access

The learner may point, use AAC, observe an adult demonstration, use tactile/model sequences, or dictate. Gripping, pushing strongly, standing, walking, or seeing fast motion is not required.

Monday evidence checkpoint

Save after the learner links a push/pull interaction to an observed motion change and distinguishes force from a substance stored in the toy.

Tuesday · Ramp Fair-Test & Measurement Lab

Change one condition, measure consistently, and repeat before claiming a pattern

Children compare ramp conditions with a controlled starting setup.

Fair-test frame

Change one planned variable at a time. Keep the same toy, starting point, release method, surface/track, measurement method, and observation area as consistent as reasonably possible.

Release, do not secretly push

If the question is about ramp height/slope, release the toy from the marked start without an extra launch push. Otherwise the test changes more than one condition.

Test → measure → repeat → compare

  1. Choose two safe low ramp conditions.
  2. Mark one common release point.
  3. Run at least two trials per condition.
  4. Measure travel distance from the same reference/end point using equal repeated units or another consistent accessible method.
  5. Record trial results.
  6. Notice whether repeated trials are identical or variable.
  7. Make a cautious evidence claim about this setup.

Steeper is not “better”

A steeper ramp may change acceleration/speed in a setup, but “better” depends on the design criterion—for example stopping safely, carrying cargo, staying on course, traveling a target distance, or accessibility. Farther is not automatically better.

Measurement integrity

Do not mix unit sizes or change the starting reference. A trial that veers sideways or collides with an obstacle is data about the setup; do not silently discard it just to make results look neat.

Tuesday evidence checkpoint

Save after the learner identifies the changed variable, preserves key controls, measures from a common reference, and uses repeated trials before claiming a result.

Wednesday · Wheels, Axles & Surface Lab

Wheels can change how objects move across surfaces—but they do not erase friction

Children compare rolling/sliding models and connect design features to evidence.

Wheel-and-axle reasoning

Wheels rotating around axles can make movement efficient in many situations by replacing much sliding contact with rolling contact. Wheels do not eliminate friction; axle friction, rolling resistance, deformation, surface texture, alignment, and other factors still matter.

Surface matters

A model may travel differently on smooth card, fabric, textured mat, or another safe surface. Do not teach “rough always means bad”—traction can be useful when steering or stopping is a criterion.

Compare one feature

  1. State a criterion: target distance, straight travel, stable cargo, controlled stop, or another safe goal.
  2. Change one feature such as surface or wheel alignment.
  3. Keep other conditions as consistent as possible.
  4. Repeat and measure/observe.
  5. Choose a design based on the stated criterion—not appearance.
  6. Revise one feature and explain why.

Engineering is tradeoffs

A design optimized for speed may not be best for stability; a surface with more traction may reduce travel distance but improve control. There is no single best transportation design for every purpose.

Failure is evidence

A wheel rubbing the body, an axle misaligned, or a vehicle leaving the path can reveal a design issue. Revision is engineering evidence, not a failure of the learner.

Wednesday evidence checkpoint

Save after the learner explains a wheel/surface effect, rejects “wheels remove friction,” and uses a stated criterion to justify or revise a model.

Thursday · Accessible Transportation System Design Lab

Transportation systems connect people/things to purposes—and access is a design criterion

Children use a fictional map/system model rather than private real travel information.

Purpose before mode

Ask what/whom needs to move, from where to where in the fictional model, with what cargo/access needs, under what safety/time/space constraints. Mode choice follows purpose and evidence.

Access is engineering

Consider ramps/lifts, curb transitions, platform gaps, seating/securement, readable/tactile/audio information, safe crossings, rest points, and route continuity as age-appropriate design ideas. Mobility aids are tools for access and movement, not symbols of helplessness.

Design a fictional route/system

  1. Use a fictional map with start, destination, obstacles, and services.
  2. Select a transportation mode/system or combination.
  3. Name at least two criteria/constraints.
  4. Include one accessibility feature.
  5. Use map/key/spatial language to communicate the route.
  6. Identify one limitation/tradeoff and revise.

No private travel disclosure

No home address, school route, bus stop, license plate, commute, immigration/travel history, car ownership, disability diagnosis, or family transportation spending is required. Fictional routes are sufficient.

System choices are contextual

Do not rank people morally by transportation mode. Geography, disability/access, infrastructure, work, caregiving, weather, safety, affordability, and available services affect choices. Week 23's system-responsibility reasoning still applies.

Thursday evidence checkpoint

Save after the learner designs/explains a fictional route or system using criteria, one accessibility feature, a map/spatial representation, and a stated tradeoff.

Friday · Engineering Evidence Conference

Explain what changed, what stayed controlled, what the evidence shows, and what should be revised

Friday consolidates Week 24 before formal mastery.

Retrieval round

  1. Name a push/pull and a resulting motion change.
  2. Explain why a ramp fair test needs controlled conditions.
  3. Explain why repeated trials matter.
  4. Correct “wheels remove all friction.”
  5. Explain why farther/faster is not always the design goal.

Repair the claims

Correct statements such as “the ramp gives the toy force,” “one trial proves it,” “steeper is always better,” “wheels have no friction,” “the vehicle that goes farthest is always best,” or “everyone should use the same transportation.”

Integrated engineering scenario

Given a toy-scale test table and fictional route: identify the changed variable and controls, interpret repeated distance/behavior evidence, select a design using stated criteria/constraints, include an accessibility feature, identify one safety/privacy boundary, and propose a revision plus evidence to test it.

Safety review

No child-riding ramps, real-road tests, traffic exposure, projectile launches, heavy rolling objects, or tests aimed at people/animals are required or authorized. A model/data demonstration fully satisfies the academic target.

Reflection

Complete through any accessible mode: “A fair test keeps…,” “Repeated trials help because…,” “A design can be better for one goal but not another because…,” or “An accessible route should include…”.

Friday evidence checkpoint

Save after the learner integrates motion, fair testing, measurement, repeated evidence, design criteria/tradeoffs, accessibility, safety, and revision.

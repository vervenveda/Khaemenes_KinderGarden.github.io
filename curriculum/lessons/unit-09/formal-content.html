Families, Homes & Traditions

Explore belonging, stories across time, homes and spaces, measurement, and meaningful traditions—without assuming that every family looks, lives, speaks, believes, or celebrates in the same way.

Essential Question: How can stories, places, routines, and traditions help people understand belonging while still respecting privacy and difference?

Week 9 Learning Arc

Monday: understand that families and caring communities can take many forms.

Tuesday: listen to and sequence an oral-history or fictional past/present story.

Wednesday: compare home/building objects using nonstandard length and height measures.

Thursday: explore traditions as meaningful repeated practices that can vary and change.

Friday: integrate story sequence, measurement, past/present, belonging, privacy, and respect.

Continuity: Week 7 used spatial language and design. Week 8 showed that repeated structures can carry meaning. Week 9 uses both ideas to notice how places, routines, and stories can be organized and remembered.

Privacy & Inclusion Boundary

No forced disclosure: a learner is never required to reveal an address, legal or biological relationship, immigration history, adoption/foster status, housing insecurity, family conflict, trauma, religion, finances, or private tradition. Fictional, historical, community, classroom, or imagined examples are always valid.

Use inclusive language such as family, caregivers, trusted adults, people who care for us, household, community. Do not assume two parents, a mother/father pair, shared surnames, one home, home ownership, siblings, grandparents, marriage, or any particular family structure.

Universal expression: stories may be shared through speech, sign, AAC, drawing, sequencing cards, objects, acting, dictation, or a home language. Personal photos are never required.

Monday · Belonging Detectives

Many ways to care and belong

Children learn that “family” and “home” are not single shapes to copy. The academic target is respectful description, comparison, and belonging—not disclosure of private family information.

Read / discuss

What can caring look like?

Use fictional picture scenarios showing different caring groups: one caregiver, several caregivers, extended family, siblings, friends/community, multigenerational households, separated households, or people living apart but staying connected.

Ask: “What caring action do you notice?” rather than “Which family is normal?”

Language

Describe without assuming

Practice words such as care, belong, household, caregiver, tradition, memory, past, present.

Apply

Belonging Web

  1. Choose a fictional character, storybook character, classroom community, or personal example only if the learner freely chooses.
  2. Place the character in the center.
  3. Add people, places, routines, pets, objects, or community spaces that help the character feel cared for or connected.
  4. Explain one connection without revealing private information.

Privacy skill: “I can say, ‘I want to use a made-up example.’” That is a valid academic choice.

Scaffold / extend

Respectful comparison

Scaffold: choose between two fictional examples. Extend: name something two families/communities can share while still being different.

Family connection

Optional home prompt

Invite—not require—a trusted adult to share a safe story about a routine, object, place, or tradition. If unavailable or unwanted, use a fictional/classroom story instead.

Monday evidence checkpoint

Save after the learner has used respectful, non-assumptive language about belonging and understands that a fictional/private alternative is always allowed.

Tuesday · Then & Now Story Lab

Oral history, memory, and sequence

Children listen to a short remembered or fictional story, place events in order, and distinguish past from present without treating memory as perfect evidence.

Mini-lesson

Past and present

Before / past→Now / present

Use visible examples: “Yesterday we built a pattern. Today we are telling a story about it.”

History thinking

Memory is a source

Explain simply: “People can remember the same event differently. An oral history tells us what someone remembers or understands. We listen respectfully and may compare it with other evidence.”

Sequence

First → Next → Last

Tell a short three-part fictional story about moving into, visiting, building, or caring for a place. Mix up three picture/text cards, then have the learner restore the order.

Ask the learner to retell the story using temporal words such as first, next, then, later, last, before, now.

Literacy

Question the storyteller

Practice one respectful question: “What happened next?” “How did you know?” or “What do you remember most?” A learner may ask about the fictional story instead of a personal family story.

Home language

Sequence works in every language

Use the learner's home language or AAC for sequencing. The goal is chronological reasoning and communication, not English-only vocabulary.

Tuesday evidence checkpoint

Save after the learner has sequenced and retold a three-part oral-history or fictional story and identified at least one past/present distinction.

Wednesday · Homes & Measure

Compare length and height fairly

Children measure and compare models or household/classroom objects without comparing the worth of homes, neighborhoods, or people's bodies.

Math

Same-size units matter

Measure two paper “homes,” block towers, books, boxes, or drawn structures with identical counters, cubes, paper clips, hand-width cards, or repeated paper units.

Teacher language: “Start at the same edge, place units end to end, and do not leave gaps or overlaps.”

Compare

Longer, shorter, taller, same

Model A

5 equal units

Model B

8 equal units

Ask: “Which is longer? How many units longer?”

Investigation

Measure a model neighborhood

  1. Build or draw at least two structures using blocks, paper, boxes, or marks.
  2. Measure length or height using one consistent nonstandard unit.
  3. Record the numbers with marks, numerals, objects, or dictation.
  4. Compare using longer/shorter/taller/same.
  5. Explain why changing the unit size could change the number counted.

Equity boundary: no house size, ownership status, neighborhood, or material is described as a better family or a more valuable person.

Access

Measurement without fine-motor barriers

An adult may position units while the learner directs placement; tactile strips, large blocks, eye-gaze choices, pointing, or AAC may be used. The target is measurement reasoning.

Connection

From Week 7 to Week 9

Week 7 described spatial relationships and structures. Today children add measurement language to those designs.

Wednesday evidence checkpoint

Save after the learner has measured with repeated equal units and made an accurate length/height comparison.

Thursday · Tradition & Change Studio

Meaningful practices can repeat and change

Children explore traditions as practices people intentionally repeat—not stereotypes every member of a group must follow.

Concept

What is a tradition?

A tradition can be a repeated way of gathering, making, remembering, helping, eating, singing, speaking, celebrating, mourning, working, creating, or caring. Some are religious; many are not. Some families keep traditions; others change, combine, pause, or do not use them.

Respect

“Some” is safer than “all”

Teach children not to guess religion, nationality, ethnicity, language, or traditions from appearance or name.

Maker project

Belonging House / Memory Window

Create a fictional, community, or voluntary personal drawing/model with four “windows”:

  1. Story: one safe memory or invented story.
  2. Place: a meaningful or imagined place.
  3. Practice: a repeated routine/tradition.
  4. Value: care, kindness, creativity, responsibility, humor, learning, or another value.

The artifact never needs names, faces, addresses, flags, religious symbols, legal relationships, or private information.

Change over time

Traditions are not frozen

Compare a fictional practice “then” and “now.” Ask what stayed the same, what changed, and why people might adapt traditions when families move, grow, gain new members, lose members, combine cultures, change beliefs, or face new circumstances.

Cultural care

Learn, don't perform stereotypes

Use books, first-person/community sources, or family-shared examples when available. Do not costume children as cultures, imitate accents, reenact sacred ceremonies, or require a child to act as spokesperson for a group.

Thursday evidence checkpoint

Save after the learner has described a tradition/routine as a repeated meaningful practice and demonstrated respectful “some, not all” thinking.

Friday · Belonging Story Circle

Retrieve, integrate, respect, reflect

Friday brings the week's ideas together before formal mastery: sequence, past/present, measurement, belonging, tradition, privacy, and respect.

Retrieval

Five quick asks

  1. Put three events in first/next/last order.
  2. Tell whether an example belongs to past or present.
  3. Measure/compare two models with equal units.
  4. Explain a tradition without saying every person in a group does it.
  5. Name one kind of private information a learner may choose not to share.
Source thinking

Story, memory, evidence

Finish: “An oral history can tell us what someone remembers. We can listen respectfully and also know that people may remember differently.”

Integrated challenge

Create a safe belonging story

  1. Choose fictional/community or voluntary personal characters.
  2. Tell a three-step past-to-present story.
  3. Include one place/model with a length or height comparison.
  4. Include one repeated practice/tradition using respectful language.
  5. State one privacy choice the storyteller controls.

Use speech, sign, AAC, drawing, sequence cards, objects, acting, dictation, or home language.

Reflect

Respect changes our language

Complete: “I can learn about another family or community by ____. I should not assume ____.”

Portfolio

Keep only safe evidence

Save a fictional story card, measurement page, belonging-house drawing, teacher note, or other artifact that does not expose private family information. Personal family photos or documents are never required.

Friday evidence checkpoint

Save only after retrieval/remediation and the integrated challenge. This confirms the learning sequence is complete; it does not award mastery.

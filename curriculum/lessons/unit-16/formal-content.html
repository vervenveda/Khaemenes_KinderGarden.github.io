Needs, Wants, Money & Choices

Reason about needs and wants in context, count and compare safe classroom tokens, distinguish goods from services, make choices under simple constraints, and learn that money is a tool—not a measure of human worth.

Essential Question: How can information, counting, priorities, and kindness help us make choices when resources are limited?

Economic Dignity & Universal Access

No poverty or wealth performance: never ask learners to reveal family income, debt, rent, food insecurity, housing status, purchases, bank use, allowance, gifts, benefits, possessions, or whether adults can afford something. No child is “better” for saving, owning more, giving more, or spending less.

Needs vary by person and circumstance. Medicine, mobility devices, communication access, culturally necessary items, transportation, safe housing, food, water, clothing, care, rest, and other supports can be needs. A teacher should not turn a simplified classroom sort into a judgment about another person's life.

Access: use counters, symbols, drawings, tactile tokens, AAC, eye gaze, partner placement, or adult scribing. Real coins, real money, reading price numerals, fine-motor coin handling, and shopping experience are not required.

Monday · Needs & Context Detectives

A need depends on what helps a person live, stay safe, participate, and be cared for

Children reason about needs/wants without treating one household's circumstances as universal.

Needs are not a shame category

Food, safe water, shelter, care, rest, health supports, clothing appropriate to conditions, communication access, and safety are examples. Individual needs can differ: glasses, medication, assistive technology, dietary supports, transportation, or other accommodations may be essential for one person and not another.

Wants can matter too

A want is not automatically foolish or bad. Play, beauty, celebration, hobbies, comfort, cultural participation, and preferred choices can add meaning. The lesson is about prioritizing under a stated constraint, not moralizing desire.

Context sort

  1. Use fictional scenario cards—not personal disclosures.
  2. Ask: “What does this person need to be safe/healthy/able to participate?”
  3. Identify a possible want or preference.
  4. Explain how context could change the answer.
  5. Use “might/could” rather than declaring what every person needs.

Opinion vs reason

Practice starters such as “I would choose ___ because ___,” “The information says ___,” and “Another person might choose differently because ___.”

Privacy rule

Fictional examples are always enough. No learner must compare their home, food, clothing, money, family purchases, or access to resources with classmates.

Monday evidence checkpoint

Save after the learner reasons about a fictional need/want choice and explains why context matters.

Tuesday · Token Count Lab

Count the amount before comparing choices

Children use one-to-one correspondence to count and compare classroom token quantities.

Tokens are math tools

Use identical paper circles, counters, bottle-cap-sized classroom manipulatives, tactile marks, or digital symbols. They are not wages, rewards for worthiness, or real currency.

Touch/move/mark each token once. State the total after the final count.

Build → count → compare

  1. Build two sets from 0–10 tokens.
  2. Count each with one-to-one correspondence.
  3. Compare using more, fewer, same, and/or numerical difference.
  4. Represent one amount with objects, drawing, marks, numeral, AAC, or tactile symbols.
  5. Check by recounting or matching pairs.

Pennies are optional

If U.S. pennies are shown, explain they are one local currency example. Coin names, currency symbols, exchange rates, and real-money handling are not required for the mathematical goal.

Amounts do not rank people

More tokens in a fictional task means a larger modeled amount—not a better person, harder worker, smarter child, or more deserving family.

Tuesday evidence checkpoint

Save after the learner counts two token sets accurately and compares their amounts with evidence.

Wednesday · Goods & Services Inquiry

People meet needs through many kinds of work, resources, relationships, and systems

Children distinguish goods from services without ranking kinds of work.

Goods

A good is a physical item that can be used, such as a book, bandage, bus part, pencil, meal, or coat. Goods require materials, labor, transport, knowledge, and other resources.

Services

A service is work/action that helps meet a need or goal, such as teaching, repairing, translating, caring, transporting, cleaning, advising, or maintaining systems. Some work is paid; some is unpaid, shared, family, volunteer, or community labor.

Classify and trace

  1. Choose a fictional scenario.
  2. Identify one good and/or service involved.
  3. State the classification rule.
  4. Connect it to a need or goal.
  5. Name one worker/resource/system that helps make it available—without stereotyping who does that work.

Money is one coordination tool

Communities also use sharing, public services, gifts, caregiving, mutual aid, household work, borrowing, repair, reuse, and other arrangements. Do not teach that every important contribution has a price.

No job hierarchy

Do not rank people by pay, prestige, uniform, education, race, gender, citizenship, disability, or whether work is paid. Study what the work does and what conditions/resources it requires.

Wednesday evidence checkpoint

Save after the learner distinguishes a good from a service and connects one example to a need/goal without stereotyping work.

Thursday · Choice Market Lab

A choice uses information, a constraint, and a reason

Children use a fictional market to practice tradeoffs, saving, and voluntary sharing without real transactions.

Fictional budget

Example: “You have 5 classroom tokens. Option A costs 2, B costs 4, and C costs 5.” Count the modeled amount, compare options, and explain a choice. Prices are invented for math practice.

Tradeoff

Choosing one option can mean delaying or not choosing another under the same constraint. A tradeoff is not proof that one option is morally superior.

Choose → explain → reconsider

  1. Count the available fictional tokens.
  2. Identify the scenario's stated priority/need.
  3. Compare at least two possible choices.
  4. Choose and explain a reason.
  5. Change one fact—price, need, time, accessibility, or quantity—and decide whether the choice should change.

Saving is delayed use, not virtue

Saving can mean setting aside resources for a later goal or uncertainty. Spending to meet a need can be responsible. A person unable to save is not irresponsible.

Sharing is voluntary

Sharing can be kind, but children are not required to give away personal possessions, food, money, or comfort items. Classroom market materials stay fictional/shared and return to the activity.

Thursday evidence checkpoint

Save after the learner makes a fictional constrained choice, explains a tradeoff, and revises the decision when one fact changes.

Friday · Decision Conference

Retrieve, count, compare, reason, and protect dignity

Friday is review and remediation before formal mastery—not a hidden test.

Retrieval round

  1. Explain why needs can depend on context.
  2. Count/compare two token sets.
  3. Classify one good and one service.
  4. Explain a tradeoff in a fictional choice.
  5. Explain why money/possessions do not measure a person's worth or responsibility.

Spot the unfair claim

Challenge statements such as “Everyone needs the same things,” “People with more money worked harder,” “Saving always means making the best choice,” or “A good person always shares.” Replace each with a more accurate, respectful statement.

Integrated decision task

Given a fictional character, a stated need/goal, and a small token amount, count the resources, compare options, distinguish any good/service involved, choose with a reason, identify a tradeoff, and explain how a changed fact might change the decision.

Remediation

If counting/comparison is unstable, return to paired objects and one-to-one correspondence. If needs/wants reasoning becomes judgmental, return to fictional context and “might/could” language.

Reflection

Ask: “What information helped you make or change a choice?” Accept speech, sign, AAC, drawing, token model, gesture, or dictation.

Friday evidence checkpoint

Save after retrieval, misconception repair as needed, and an integrated economic-choice explanation.

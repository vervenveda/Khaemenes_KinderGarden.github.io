Gratitude, Giving & Storytelling

Retell stories with beginning, middle, and end; compose and decompose numbers to 5; notice acts of care; and choose authentic ways to respond—without forcing feelings, affection, gifts, money, religion, or private disclosure.

Essential Question: How can stories, number ideas, and chosen actions help us notice and respond to care?

Universal Access & Emotional Safety

Appreciation can be spoken, signed, drawn, dictated, written, shown with AAC, acted through a helpful choice, or privately reflected. A learner may use fictional/community examples.

No forced gratitude: a child is never required to hug, kiss, touch, praise, forgive, give an object, spend money, disclose hardship, name a benefactor, participate in religion, or say “thank you” for something that felt unsafe or unwanted. Gratitude is not a debt.
Access: speech, sign, AAC, gesture, picture sequencing, tactile objects, home language, dictation, and adult scribing are valid. Fine-motor skill and conventional spelling are not the mastery target.

Monday · Story Structure Detectives

A retelling keeps the important events in a sensible order

Children identify beginning, middle, and end while distinguishing a retelling from an exact memorized copy.

Three-part structure

Beginning: who/where/problem or situation. Middle: important action/change. End: result, response, or stopping point. Real stories can be more complex, but this structure supports Kindergarten retelling.

Retell, don't recite

A strong retelling preserves important meaning and sequence; it does not require exact wording, eye contact, speech, or remembering every detail.

Sequence a care story

  1. Use three fictional picture cards or teacher-read events.
  2. Place them in a sensible order.
  3. Retell with first/next/last, beginning/middle/end, or equivalent home-language temporal words.
  4. Explain one clue that helped establish the order.

Perspective

Two people may focus on different important details. Ask what evidence in the story supports each retelling.

Privacy option

No personal family event is required. Fictional stories, public stories, or classroom-created examples are fully valid.

Monday evidence checkpoint

Save after the learner sequences and retells a three-part story with one relevant supporting detail.

Tuesday · Number Story Lab

Five can be made and taken apart in different ways

Children compose and decompose quantities to 5 using objects, drawings, fingers, movement, tactile pieces, or symbols.

Part-part-whole

5 = 0 + 5
5 = 1 + 4
5 = 2 + 3
5 = 3 + 2
5 = 4 + 1

Children do not need formal equation notation to demonstrate the concept. The goal is seeing a whole as parts that can be recombined.

Giving is not always subtraction

A “giving” story may move objects between people while the total stays the same. A “used/taken away” story may reduce a set. Keep the mathematical action explicit rather than tying one social word to one operation.

Build, split, explain

  1. Build a set from 1–5.
  2. Split it into two groups.
  3. Say/show the two parts and the whole.
  4. Recombine and verify the total.
  5. Try a different split for the same whole.

Number story

Create a fictional story such as: “There are 5 library cards. 2 are on one table and 3 are on another. How many altogether?” Then model the parts.

Equity boundary

Do not use a child's access to food, toys, money, gifts, or possessions as evidence of generosity or mathematical competence.

Tuesday evidence checkpoint

Save after the learner composes or decomposes a whole to 5 in at least two ways and explains the parts/whole relationship.

Wednesday · Care & Community Inquiry

Care can be visible in many kinds of work and action

Children examine evidence of helping while avoiding “hero” stereotypes or ranking some community roles as more worthy than others.

Notice the action

Examples may include a sanitation worker removing waste, a caregiver preparing medicine, a neighbor sharing information, a librarian organizing resources, a child returning materials, or someone repairing a walkway.

Community helpers are people, not costumes

Do not stereotype jobs by gender, race, class, uniform, disability, or nationality. People may perform paid, unpaid, formal, informal, family, volunteer, or community work.

Action → need → effect

  1. Choose a fictional/community care example.
  2. Name the action you can observe or reasonably infer from the scenario.
  3. Name the need it addresses.
  4. Name one possible effect—and one thing you would need more evidence to know.

Helping has boundaries

A child does not owe help that is unsafe, secret, inappropriate, or beyond their ability. “Get a trusted adult” is often the responsible action.

Gratitude is optional emotion

Children may notice a helpful action without being required to feel a particular emotion. Respectful acknowledgment and truthful reflection are sufficient.

Wednesday evidence checkpoint

Save after the learner connects a care action to a need/effect and demonstrates one safety or boundary principle.

Thursday · Appreciation Message Studio

Choose a truthful, specific response

Children communicate appreciation or acknowledgment without forced affection, spending, disclosure, or performance.

Specific beats generic

Model: “Thank you for helping me find the book” or “I noticed you held the door” rather than requiring exaggerated praise. If “thank you” does not fit, a factual acknowledgment or private reflection is valid.

Recipient choice

A message may be for a fictional character, classroom/community role, family member, friend, self, place, shared resource, or no recipient at all. Sending it is optional.

Create a message with meaning

  1. Name or depict the action/event.
  2. Communicate what was helpful, meaningful, interesting, or noticed.
  3. Choose speech, sign, AAC, drawing, dictation, writing, collage, or another accessible format.
  4. Review whether the message is truthful and respects privacy/boundaries.

No gift requirement

Materials can be scrap paper or no physical material at all. Appreciation is never measured by money, purchased gifts, artistic polish, or how much a child gives away.

Consent check

Do not require hugs, kisses, touching, public speaking, eye contact, forgiveness, or contact with a person the learner does not want to contact.

Thursday evidence checkpoint

Save after the learner creates or explains a truthful appreciation/acknowledgment response that respects consent and privacy.

Friday · Story & Number Conference

Retrieve, explain, correct, and prepare for mastery

Friday integrates narrative structure, number composition, care reasoning, truthful appreciation, and boundaries.

Five retrieval prompts

  1. Retell beginning-middle-end.
  2. Show two ways to make or split a number to 5.
  3. Explain one community care action and need.
  4. Name one appreciation choice that does not require money or touch.
  5. Name one helping/gratitude boundary.

Misconception clinic

Correct: “giving always means subtracting,” “a good thank-you must be a gift,” “you must hug someone to show gratitude,” “all helpers wear uniforms,” or “if someone helps you, you owe them.”

Integrated story

Create or retell a short fictional story with beginning-middle-end that contains a quantity to 5 and an act of care. Show the number relationship and explain a truthful, consent-respecting response.

Reflection

Name one idea that became clearer and one way to notice care without making assumptions about another person's feelings or circumstances.

Mastery boundary

Finishing Friday does not itself award mastery. Formal Week 13 mastery still requires at least 80% overall plus every required core criterion.

Friday evidence checkpoint

Save after retrieval, misconception repair, and an integrated story/number/boundary explanation.

Week 13 daily learning complete

Daily evidence is complete; it does not award mastery. Continue to the adult-observed performance rubric.

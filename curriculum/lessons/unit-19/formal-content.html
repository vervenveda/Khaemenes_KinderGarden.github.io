A++++ Kindergarten · Week 19

Goals, Strategies & Learning Growth

Use evidence to notice personal learning growth, choose a small academic goal, compare strategies, practice forward and backward number sequences, ask for support, and revise the plan when a strategy or condition is not working.

Essential Question: How can evidence, strategies, support, and revision help us learn something that is not secure yet?

Daily evidence: 0 of 5

Daily evidence unlocks the next day; it does not award mastery.

Week 19 Learning Arc

Monday: compare the learner’s own past/current evidence and describe change without peer ranking.

Tuesday: design a small, observable learning goal and identify strategies/supports.

Wednesday: investigate forward/backward number sequences and compare valid strategies without speed pressure.

Thursday: test a strategy, notice evidence, ask for support, and revise the goal/plan when needed.

Friday: integrate evidence, goal, strategy, math reasoning, correction, and next-step reflection.

Semester-two handoff: Week 19 can open only after qualifying midyear mastery. The new semester begins with evidence from learning—not a reset of the learner’s identity or an assumption that a calendar date creates growth.

Mindset, Privacy & Access Commitments

No calendar requirement: no New Year celebration, resolution, religious/cultural observance, January timing, or particular calendar is required. The lesson works whenever Week 19 occurs.
Growth is not a character score. Effort alone is not a universal solution. Learning can depend on strategy, instruction, tools, accommodations, practice, feedback, rest, environment, health, time, and access. Disability, language difference, trauma, poverty, fatigue, or access barriers are not failures of mindset.
Access: “I need help,” “I need another way,” “I need a break,” and “this goal should change” are valid learning statements. Speech, sign, AAC, pictures, tactile models, dictation, adult scribing, manipulatives, and additional processing time are valid pathways.

No public goal chart: goals may be private between learner and authorized adult. No body, weight, food, sleep, medical, family-finance, religious, trauma, home-behavior, or other sensitive personal goal is required.

Monday · Growth Evidence Detectives

Compare yourself with earlier evidence—not with another learner

Children use work samples, adult notes, models, or remembered strategies to notice a real change in their own learning.

Then / now evidence

Choose a safe academic example: a pattern that is easier to explain, a longer count that stays one-to-one, a clearer story sequence, more accurate measurement, stronger help-seeking, or a science idea that was revised.

Growth is not always a straight line

Performance can vary by day, context, fatigue, language demand, sensory conditions, unfamiliar materials, or support. One difficult attempt does not erase prior learning; one easy attempt does not prove every related skill is secure.

Evidence → claim → limit

  1. Look at or remember one earlier learning example.
  2. Look at or complete one current comparable example.
  3. Name what changed.
  4. Give one piece of evidence for the claim.
  5. Name one thing this comparison does not prove.

No peer ranking

Do not compare who learned faster, who needs fewer supports, whose handwriting looks neater, or who is “ahead.” Growth evidence is used to choose a next teaching move.

Truthful language

“I can…,” “I am learning…,” “I need help with…,” and “I do not understand this yet” are all valid. Do not force positive self-talk that contradicts the learner’s actual experience.

Monday evidence checkpoint

Save after the learner compares personal past/current academic evidence and makes one supported growth or next-step claim without peer ranking.

Tuesday · Goal & Strategy Design Lab

A useful goal describes learning we can observe and support

Children turn an evidence-based next step into a small academic goal and a flexible strategy plan.

Goal, not identity label

Prefer “I will count a set to 10 by touching each object once” over “I will be good at math.” Prefer “I will retell three events in order” over “I will be a better reader.” The goal describes a skill, not the child’s worth.

Plan supports up front

List at least two possible supports or strategies: counters, number path, picture sequence, model, home-language explanation, AAC, adult prompt, quieter space, larger materials, tactile symbols, worked example, break, or more practice time.

Evidence → goal → strategy

  1. Choose a non-sensitive academic target from Monday.
  2. Describe what success would look/sound/feel like in an observable way.
  3. Choose one first strategy.
  4. Choose a backup support/strategy.
  5. Decide what evidence would tell you to keep, change, pause, or revise the goal.

Goals can change

A goal may be changed when new evidence shows it is too broad, too easy, too difficult, poorly defined, inaccessible, or no longer useful. Revising a goal is reasoning—not failure.

Privacy boundary

No public goal chart, behavior confession, family disclosure, health/body goal, religious resolution, financial goal, or home compliance target is required. Fictional/sample goals may be used for practice.

Tuesday evidence checkpoint

Save after the learner creates or interprets an observable academic goal with at least two possible strategies/supports and a revision condition.

Wednesday · Number Strategy Lab

Number sequence can be represented in more than one valid way

Children count forward and backward in a developmentally appropriate range and compare strategies without treating speed as mastery.

Forward from a number

Start from a provided number rather than always beginning at 1. Continue using a number path, counters, fingers when appropriate, spoken/signed sequence, AAC selection, visual cards, tactile markers, or another consistent representation.

Backward is a sequence skill

Counting backward is not subtraction. It can support later subtraction reasoning, but saying a decreasing number sequence does not by itself demonstrate taking-away meaning.

Two strategies, one relationship

  1. Choose an appropriate short forward sequence and represent it.
  2. Start from another number and continue forward.
  3. Represent a short backward sequence.
  4. Try the same sequence with a different representation.
  5. Compare which strategy helped and why.

Speed is not the goal.

A learner may pause, point, use a number path, sign, use AAC, move counters, or self-correct. Fast recitation with lost cardinality/sequence understanding is not stronger evidence than careful accurate reasoning.

Strategy comparison

Two strategies can both be valid. “More efficient” depends on the task, learner, access needs, and purpose; do not teach one body-dependent or speech-dependent method as universally best.

Wednesday evidence checkpoint

Save after the learner represents forward and backward number sequences and compares two strategies without a speed requirement.

Thursday · Try, Notice & Revise Lab

Effort is one input; strategy and conditions matter too

Children test a goal strategy, collect simple evidence, seek support when useful, and revise rather than repeating an ineffective approach indefinitely.

Try with a purpose

Use the Tuesday goal or a fictional academic goal. Try the selected strategy once or twice under a reasonable condition. Record what helped, what remained difficult, and what evidence was produced.

Do not prescribe “try harder”

Effort alone is not a universal solution. When a strategy is not working, possible next moves include clearer instruction, a different representation, smaller step, assistive tool, more accessible material, feedback, rest/break, extra time, environmental change, or a revised goal.

Plan → try → notice → choose

  1. Name the goal.
  2. Name the strategy/support.
  3. Try a brief task.
  4. Notice evidence—not feelings alone.
  5. Choose: continue, change strategy, ask for help, take a break, change conditions, or revise the goal.
  6. Explain the choice.

Access is not cheating

Accommodations and assistive supports remove barriers. The evidence question is whether the learner demonstrates the intended concept—not whether they did it without tools.

Persistence includes stopping safely

Persistence does not mean continuing through pain, overwhelm, unsafe conditions, or repeated failure without new instruction. Pausing and seeking help can be skilled self-management.

Thursday evidence checkpoint

Save after the learner tests a strategy, uses evidence to decide a next move, and demonstrates that revision/help/access can be part of effective learning.

Friday · Learning Growth Conference

Explain the evidence, strategy, correction, and next step

Friday integrates goal reasoning and number strategy work before formal Week 19 mastery.

Retrieval round

  1. Name one personal learning change supported by evidence.
  2. Describe one observable academic goal.
  3. Name two possible strategies/supports.
  4. Represent a forward and short backward number sequence.
  5. Explain when changing strategy/help/access is wiser than simply repeating the same attempt.

Repair the claims

Correct statements such as “smart children do not need help,” “if you try hard enough every barrier disappears,” “the fastest counter understands best,” “a goal can never change,” or “accommodations make the work less real.”

Integrated growth task

Given a fictional learning sample, identify evidence, choose a small skill goal, select a strategy/support, demonstrate a related number-sequence task, notice a mistake or barrier, and revise the plan. Explain what evidence would show progress next time.

Dignity boundary

Growth is not a character score. A child is not more worthy, disciplined, intelligent, or deserving because they learn faster, need fewer accommodations, speak more confidently, or reach a goal sooner.

Reflection

Use any accessible mode to complete: “One strategy that helps me learn is…”, “One kind of help I can ask for is…”, or “One thing I would change after seeing evidence is…”.

Friday evidence checkpoint

Save after the learner integrates evidence, an observable goal, strategy/support choice, number-sequence reasoning, and evidence-based revision.

Week 19 daily learning is complete

The learner may now open the transparent adult-observed mastery rubric. Daily receipts document learning experiences; they do not award mastery.

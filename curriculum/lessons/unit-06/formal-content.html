Weather, Sun & Sky

Children become careful weather observers: they describe what they can actually notice, build and interpret simple weather data, investigate sunlight and shade safely, make weather-ready decisions, and communicate evidence like young scientists.

Essential Question: How does weather affect what people, plants, animals, and communities do?

Carry Week 5 observation into the sky.

Access: learners may use local observations, an adult-provided photo, a safe indoor view, tactile/symbol cards, speech, sign, AAC, drawing, dictation, or any home language. A particular climate, outdoor space, or material is never required.
☀️ Non-negotiable Sun & Weather Safety

Never look directly at the Sun, even through sunglasses, clouds, cameras, binoculars, magnifiers, or improvised filters. Observe light and shade on safe surfaces, not the Sun itself. Never send a child outdoors to observe lightning, severe storms, dangerous heat/cold, smoke, flooding, high winds, or other hazardous conditions. Follow trusted adults and local emergency/weather authorities.

Day 1 · Observe + Describe

Monday · Sky Watchers

Objective: Describe current weather with accurate vocabulary, separate observation from inference, and create one shared weather sentence.

Teacher model

Say only what the evidence supports

  1. Look through a safe window or use an adult-provided weather image.
  2. Think aloud: “I see gray clouds moving. I hear rain on the roof.”
  3. Contrast with a guess: “I cannot know the cloud is angry. That is a story idea, not a weather observation.”
  4. Build a shared sentence: “Today is cloudy and windy.”
Vocabulary + movement

Act out weather words

Stretch tall for sunny, sway for windy, tap fingers for rainy, curl gently for cold, fan for hot. Add locally meaningful words such as foggy, humid, snowy, dry, dusty, smoky, or stormy only when safe and relevant.

Scaffold: “I notice ___.” Extension: add “because I see/hear/feel ___.”

Day 2 · Count + Represent Data

Tuesday · Weather Data Garden

Objective: Read a simple five-day weather data set, count totals, compare categories, and explain what the graph shows without pretending it predicts the future.

Teacher model

From observations to a graph

  1. Each day's weather observation becomes one data mark.
  2. Count each category carefully, one mark at a time.
  3. Compare with more, fewer, and same.
  4. Say what the graph shows: “Cloudy happened most in this sample.”
  5. Do not overclaim: five past days do not guarantee tomorrow.

Day 3 · Safe Investigation

Wednesday · Sunlight & Shade Lab

Objective: Compare light and shade safely, identify a shadow as evidence that light is blocked, and explain one way shade can affect a place or object.

☀️ Safety reminder: Never look directly at the Sun. Do not use magnifiers, binoculars, cameras, mirrors, dark plastic, homemade filters, or sunglasses to stare at it. Today's investigation looks only at lighted and shaded surfaces.
Safe materials
lamp-lit room OR daylight near a windowopaque object/bookpaperpencil

Teacher model

  1. Place a safe opaque object where light reaches a surface.
  2. Notice the darker area: the object blocks some light.
  3. Move the object or light source only if safe; notice whether the shadow changes.
  4. Compare a bright area and shaded area without touching anything dangerously hot.
Real-world connection

Why shade matters

Shade can help people, animals, plants, buildings, playgrounds, or parked objects receive less direct sunlight. What is comfortable or safe depends on local temperature, climate, health needs, and adult guidance.

Accessibility: use tactile object placement, spoken descriptions, high-contrast shapes, or an adult demonstration if visual comparison is difficult.

⛱ Light & Shade Reasoning

1. What makes a shadow?
2. Safe Sun rule?
3. What can we compare?

Use the investigation and safety rule.

Day 4 · Apply + Design

Thursday · Weather-Ready Designers

Objective: Match clothing/actions to weather conditions using reasoning—not stereotypes—and create a simple weather wheel or accessible weather-choice tool.

Teacher model

Ask what the conditions require

  1. Read the weather condition.
  2. Name the need: stay dry, stay warm, stay cool, seek shade, stay indoors, listen to an adult.
  3. Choose an action or clothing option that fits the need.
  4. Explain why. Different cultures and families may choose different appropriate clothing.
Maker project

Weather Wheel

Use a paper plate, reused cardboard, folded paper, spinner, cards, tactile symbols, or a simple four-section drawing. Include at least four locally meaningful weather conditions and one safe choice for each.

No-cost alternative: fold one sheet into four boxes and draw a symbol in each. No printer, craft kit, or purchased materials are required.

🧥 Weather-Ready Choices

Heavy rain
Very hot, strong sunlight
Lightning nearby

Choose the safe response for each condition.

Day 5 · Retrieve + Communicate

Friday · Weather Reporter

Objective: Retrieve the week's core science, data, vocabulary, and safety ideas; communicate a short evidence-based weather report; and identify anything needing remediation before mastery.

Reporter routine

Observe → Data → Reason → Safety

A weather reporter can say: “Today I notice ___. Our graph showed ___. One safe choice is ___ because ___.” Children may speak, sign, use AAC, dictate, draw a sequence, or communicate in a home language.

Important science habit

A forecast is not a promise

Weather can change. Kindergarten learners should distinguish a present observation from a prediction and know that adults/local weather authorities provide safety guidance during hazardous conditions.

🎙 Five Weather Retrievals

1. Which is a direct observation?

2. A graph shows cloudy = 3 and sunny = 1. Which happened more?

3. What creates a shadow?

4. What is the safest rule about the Sun?

5. Weather around the world…

Try all five. Four correct shows readiness for the reporting/reflection step; retry is always allowed.

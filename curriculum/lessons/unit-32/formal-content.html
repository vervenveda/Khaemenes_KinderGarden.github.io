Monday: Water Worlds Habitat Detectives

Daily learning target: Identify a pond, river, and ocean from original illustrations, describe one observable difference between their environments, and ask one question about how an animal finds what it needs.

Recall & vocabulary: Tell or point to a place where you have seen water. Key words: habitat, pond, river, ocean, observe. A picture shows clues, but does not show everything about a real place.

Morning Circle: Welcome song, visual schedule, feelings check-in, and question of the day: “How is water a home?”

Literacy: Water habitat vocabulary

Math: Sort/count water animals

Inquiry: Habitats and resources

SEL: Care and safety near water

Original guided activity: Show three educator-drawn habitat scenes: a still freshwater pond with reeds, a flowing freshwater river with banks, and a broad saltwater ocean with waves. Point out that ponds and rivers are commonly freshwater while oceans contain saltwater, without suggesting that all water animals live in all three. Invite children to sort original paper scene cards, describe one difference using words such as still, flowing, or salty, and place a matching habitat label. Model the difference between what an illustration shows and what we infer, then ask “What might an animal find here to help it live?” Use pictures only: no open water, water tubs, tasting, wildlife handling, or outdoor shoreline activity is needed.

Practice evidence (not formal mastery): Learner correctly names or indicates two of the three illustrated water habitats, describes one visible difference, and asks or selects one relevant habitat question. Support with two picture choices and modeled vocabulary; extend by comparing all three environments and naming a clue that would help an animal find food or shelter. Accept speech, drawing, pointing, or accessible communication; do not restart a completed activity to provide feedback.

Centers: Teacher table, Math/manipulative bin, Art/maker station, Dramatic play, Movement/music, Book corner, Calm reflection spot

Teacher evidence: Adult observation, child explanation, work sample, oral language, cooperative play, and assessment item.

Teach · Practice · Show independently

Adult modeling: Show a calm pond drawing next to a river drawing. Say: “These reeds and this still-looking surface are visible; whether the water is salty cannot be learned from a picture alone. The labels tell us these are freshwater scenes.”

Guided practice (three different examples):

  1. Point to reeds and a still surface in the pond; describe two features without claiming the picture proves every pond is always still.
  2. Point to current arrows and banks in the river drawing; contrast a flowing illustration with the pond.
  3. Show an ocean scene labeled saltwater. Ask what is visible (waves), and what is supplied information (salt content).

Fresh independent transfer: Present two unfamiliar original illustrations: a narrow stream with a flow arrow and a sheltered pond with floating leaves. Ask the child to identify each, explain one pictured clue, and ask one testable question about animal needs.

Corrective feedback: When a child says salty water can be seen, distinguish visible waves from the saltwater label; invite the child to rephrase without penalty.

Accessible evidence: Record the learner's own explanation, drawing, pointing, tactile model, or supported communication; this practice is not the formal 80% mastery decision.

Tuesday: Water Animal Sorting and Counting Lab

Daily learning target: Sort six original animal picture cards by a supported freshwater or saltwater habitat, count each group accurately, and explain one animal-habitat match using a visible or known clue.

Recall & vocabulary: Recall how to sort and count picture cards. Key words: freshwater, saltwater, group, count, tally. Use the labels or story clues when water type cannot be seen.

Morning Circle: Welcome song, visual schedule, feelings check-in, and question of the day: “How is water a home?”

Literacy: Water habitat vocabulary

Math: Sort/count water animals

Inquiry: Habitats and resources

SEL: Care and safety near water

Original guided activity: Prepare six original paper picture cards: two pond frogs, two river trout, and two ocean clownfish. Display three original habitat drawings labeled pond, river, and ocean. Explain that the pictured frogs are commonly found in freshwater ponds, trout in freshwater rivers, and clownfish in warm saltwater seas; real species and habitats vary. Children place each card in its illustrated habitat, count using one-to-one correspondence, and make a three-column counter graph with one counter for each card. Ask which groups contain equal numbers, then regroup the same cards into freshwater (four) and saltwater (two) and explain why the totals changed. Use pictures only; no water basin, tasting, handling animals, or external application is needed.

Practice evidence (not formal mastery): Learner matches at least four of six picture cards to the modeled habitats, counts one category correctly, and states or indicates which of the freshwater and saltwater groups has more cards. Accept speech, pointing, drawings, and accessible communication. Support with four cards and two labeled habitat choices; extend by predicting how the graph changes if one additional ocean card is introduced. Correct gently with a fresh example without restarting prior work.

Centers: Teacher table, Math/manipulative bin, Art/maker station, Dramatic play, Movement/music, Book corner, Calm reflection spot

Teacher evidence: Adult observation, child explanation, work sample, oral language, cooperative play, and assessment item.

Teach · Practice · Show independently

Adult modeling: Place three frog cards beside the pond label, touching one card per counter and counting one, two, three. Move each counted card to a completed pile to prevent double-counting.

Guided practice (three different examples):

  1. Sort two trout pictures into the flowing freshwater river, using the cards' given habitat information.
  2. Count two clownfish picture cards into the ocean column, pointing to each once.
  3. Regroup four freshwater cards and two saltwater cards; explain why the grouping rule changed but total card count did not.

Fresh independent transfer: Give a new set of seven labeled picture cards: three trout, one frog, three clownfish. Without a model graph, sort by fresh versus salt water, create a simple counter graph, and explain which group has more.

Corrective feedback: If the child counts the same card twice, physically move counted cards and retry with a different small set; avoid treating memorized animal names alone as habitat evidence.

Accessible evidence: Record the learner's own explanation, drawing, pointing, tactile model, or supported communication; this practice is not the formal 80% mastery decision.

Wednesday: Water Habitat Investigators

Daily learning target: Compare illustrated pond, river, and ocean habitats, identify one resource an aquatic animal needs, and explain a reasonable habitat prediction using a picture clue.

Recall & vocabulary: Remember a clue that helps explain where an animal may find food or shelter. Key words: resource, need, clue, predict, evidence.

Morning Circle: Welcome song, visual schedule, feelings check-in, and question of the day: “How is water a home?”

Literacy: Water habitat vocabulary

Math: Sort/count water animals

Inquiry: Habitats and resources

SEL: Care and safety near water

Original guided activity: Draw three original habitat cards: a pond with calm freshwater, plants, and a shallow edge; a river with flowing freshwater, rocks, and a bank; and an ocean with saltwater, waves, and underwater shelter. Add paper clue cards showing plants, shelter, and food. Ask learners what they can actually see in each illustration, then predict where the pictured pond frog, river trout, or ocean clownfish could find suitable conditions. Discuss that animals need appropriate water conditions, food, and shelter and that a drawing offers clues but does not prove exactly where an animal lives. Invite children to adjust one prediction after a new clue is revealed. Use paper scenes only; no open water, real animals, or shoreline visit is required.

Practice evidence (not formal mastery): Learner describes one observable difference between two habitats, chooses one plausible animal-habitat pairing, and supports it with a visible clue or modeled habitat need. Accept speaking, pointing, drawing, or accessible communication. Support with two large illustrated habitat choices; extend by asking which new observation could test a prediction. Reteach through a different illustration without restarting completed work.

Centers: Teacher table, Math/manipulative bin, Art/maker station, Dramatic play, Movement/music, Book corner, Calm reflection spot

Teacher evidence: Adult observation, child explanation, work sample, oral language, cooperative play, and assessment item.

Teach · Practice · Show independently

Adult modeling: Point to the pond's plants, the river's rocks and the ocean's underwater shelter. Say: “These clues suggest possible protection and food, but this drawing does not prove a particular animal is present.”

Guided practice (three different examples):

  1. Describe the visible shelter in a pond scene and ask what an animal could use it for.
  2. Compare two river drawings, one with rocks and one without, and predict a possible shelter difference.
  3. Given a clownfish picture labeled as an ocean species, identify which labeled habitat has appropriate saltwater and what other resource must be available.

Fresh independent transfer: Show a different original freshwater marsh drawing with reeds and shallow water, plus a labeled frog picture. Ask for one clue, one habitat prediction, and one piece of evidence that would help confirm the prediction.

Corrective feedback: If a prediction is stated as fact, prompt “What do you see?” followed by “What might that mean?” and let the learner revise.

Accessible evidence: Record the learner's own explanation, drawing, pointing, tactile model, or supported communication; this practice is not the formal 80% mastery decision.

Thursday: My Water Worlds Field Guide

Daily learning target: Create an original illustrated guide page for one water habitat, label two accurate environmental features, and explain how a suitable animal might find one resource there.

Recall & vocabulary: Recall one way people can stay safe near water. Key words: habitat guide, shelter, food, water, adult supervision. Never go near or into water alone.

Morning Circle: Welcome song, visual schedule, feelings check-in, and question of the day: “How is water a home?”

Literacy: Water habitat vocabulary

Math: Sort/count water animals

Inquiry: Habitats and resources

SEL: Care and safety near water

Original guided activity: Fold one paper page into four sections labeled “Habitat,” “What I notice,” “Animal,” and “What it needs.” Learners choose a pond, river, or ocean and draw the habitat using original classroom picture cards from earlier lessons. Model two accurate labels, such as reeds and calm freshwater for a pond, rocks and flowing freshwater for a river, or waves and saltwater for an ocean. Add a paper drawing of a suitable animal: a pond frog, river trout, or ocean clownfish. Invite children to dictate, write, or indicate one reason that the pictured environment could help their animal find food, shelter, or appropriate water conditions. Compare the guide against the reference drawing, make one evidence-based revision, and share voluntarily with a partner or educator. Keep all activities on paper; no live aquatic animals, open water, or external resources are needed.

Practice evidence (not formal mastery): Learner includes an identifiable habitat, two correct environmental labels, and an animal-resource explanation that matches the modeled scene; then identifies one intentional revision. Accept drawing, dictated words, pointing, tactile or picture choices, and accessible communication. Support with a partially completed four-part organizer; extend by comparing a second habitat and explaining a different resource or condition. The guide is optional portfolio evidence, not a formal mastery decision.

Centers: Teacher table, Math/manipulative bin, Art/maker station, Dramatic play, Movement/music, Book corner, Calm reflection spot

Teacher evidence: Adult observation, child explanation, work sample, oral language, cooperative play, and assessment item.

Teach · Practice · Show independently

Adult modeling: Fold one page into four labeled sections. Draw a river with banks and rocks; label “flowing freshwater” as supplied information and “rocks” as a visible clue. Explain a trout may use suitable water and shelter.

Guided practice (three different examples):

  1. Select a new habitat and identify two accurate features from its reference scene.
  2. Choose a labeled animal and connect its known needs to one habitat resource.
  3. Make an evidence-based sentence and revise one feature after checking the original illustration.

Fresh independent transfer: Without viewing the adult's river example, create a pond or ocean page containing two correct labels, one suitable animal, and a reason linking an animal need to a clue or labeled condition.

Corrective feedback: When a habitat label and illustration disagree, revisit which is observed and which is provided as background information; correct the label before storing the sample.

Accessible evidence: Record the learner's own explanation, drawing, pointing, tactile model, or supported communication; this practice is not the formal 80% mastery decision.

Friday: Water Worlds Reflection Circle

Daily learning target: Recognize a pond, river, and ocean in unfamiliar illustrations, justify one animal-habitat match using a relevant clue, and describe one improvement to a field-guide page.

Recall & vocabulary: Tell or show how a picture can support an idea but cannot prove everything. Key words: compare, model, observe, predict, limitation.

Morning Circle: Welcome song, visual schedule, feelings check-in, and question of the day: “How is water a home?”

Literacy: Water habitat vocabulary

Math: Sort/count water animals

Inquiry: Habitats and resources

SEL: Care and safety near water

Original guided activity: Present three fresh educator-drawn scenes: a still pond with lily pads, a flowing rocky river, and a wide ocean with coral habitat. Invite children to match original drawings of a pond frog, river trout, and ocean clownfish to the environments, using picture clues and remembering that real animals and habitats can vary. Show six paper animal cards, two per pictured habitat, and count how many belong in each of three groups using one-to-one correspondence. Ask children to explain how one matched habitat offers suitable water conditions, food, or shelter. Let each learner revisit Thursday’s original field guide, correct or clarify one label, and celebrate an observation they can now explain. All activities use paper scenes without open water or live animals; sharing is optional. Keep the existing separate weekly mastery assessment unchanged.

Practice evidence (not formal mastery): Learner identifies at least two of the three new habitats, provides a reasonable animal-habitat explanation grounded in one pictured clue or modeled need, counts one small group accurately, and communicates one field-guide revision. Accept drawing, pointing, speech, dictation, or accessible communication. Support with two habitat choices and labeled hints; extend by comparing freshwater and saltwater grouping totals. Use fresh examples for reteaching without resetting previous work; this reflection does not generate a formal mastery receipt.

Centers: Teacher table, Math/manipulative bin, Art/maker station, Dramatic play, Movement/music, Book corner, Calm reflection spot

Teacher evidence: Adult observation, child explanation, work sample, oral language, cooperative play, and assessment item.

Teach · Practice · Show independently

Adult modeling: Using an unfamiliar ocean scene with coral and waves, say: “The label identifies saltwater. Coral is visible. A clownfish needs a suitable marine environment, but a picture alone cannot confirm one lives here.”

Guided practice (three different examples):

  1. Identify pond versus river from two new illustrations and justify the choice with one visible feature.
  2. Count and regroup six new animal cards using a different order from Tuesday, explaining the one-to-one count.
  3. Compare a freshwater and saltwater scene and practice a cautious animal-resource explanation together.

Fresh independent transfer: Present new paper cards depicting a freshwater pond, a rocky flowing stream, and a coral reef. Ask the learner to match two labeled animals, give one accurate evidence-based reason, count the cards in one group, and independently revise a field-guide label.

Corrective feedback: If a child guesses based only on the animal's color, emphasize habitat-label evidence and the animal's known needs; use a new picture to check transfer.

Accessible evidence: Record the learner's own explanation, drawing, pointing, tactile model, or supported communication; this practice is not the formal 80% mastery decision.

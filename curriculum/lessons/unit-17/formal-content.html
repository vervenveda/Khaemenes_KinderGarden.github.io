Light, Shadows & Seasonal Sky

Investigate how light, objects, position, and distance affect shadows; measure changes fairly; model day and night with Earth’s rotation; and compare seasonal-light observations without assuming winter means the same weather everywhere.

Essential Question: What evidence can light and shadow give us about position, change, and patterns in our sky?

Week 17 Learning Arc

Monday: identify light sources and explain how an opaque object can block light to form a shadow.

Continuity: Week 6 introduced sunlight and safe shadow observation; Week 15 strengthened common-start measurement and consistent units. Week 17 joins those ideas in a controlled light-and-shadow investigation.

Safety, Global Accuracy & Access

Light safety: Never look directly at the Sun. Never use lasers, flames/candles, high-heat lamps, electrical disassembly, unsafe darkness, or bright lights aimed into eyes. Indoor shadows may use a cool, ordinary adult-approved light source or a teacher-created model.
No single-season stereotype: “Winter” does not mean snow, freezing temperatures, or short days in the same way everywhere. Seasons differ by latitude, hemisphere, elevation, ocean influence, and local climate. Use local observations or fictional/model data rather than universal claims.
Access: no standing outdoors, vision-only task, flashlight handling, fine-motor tracing, or spoken explanation is required. Use tactile raised lines, high-contrast cards, safe models, AAC, sign, partner placement, audio description, drawing, or adult scribing.

Monday · Light Source Detectives

A shadow needs light, an object, and a place for blocked light to be noticed

Children distinguish a light source from an object that reflects or blocks light and build an evidence-based shadow explanation.

Source or not?

A lamp or the Sun emits light. A book, wall, toy, or the Moon is not treated as its own visible-light source in this Kindergarten model; it can reflect light. Use “light source,” “opaque/block,” “transparent/transmit,” and “shadow” carefully without requiring advanced optics vocabulary.

Shadow relationship

When an opaque object blocks some light from reaching a surface, a darker region can appear. The shadow is not a substance painted onto the surface and does not come out of the object.

Source → object → screen

  1. Use an adult-approved safe light or teacher diagram.
  2. Identify the source, blocking object, and screen/surface.
  3. Predict where the shadow should appear.
  4. Observe/model and compare with the prediction.
  5. Say/show what evidence supports the explanation.

Observation vs explanation

“The dark shape is behind the card” is an observation. “The card blocked light reaching that area” is an explanation. Practice keeping both.

Sun boundary

Never look directly at the Sun to study light or shadows. Outdoor shadow evidence, if used at all, is gathered by looking at the ground/object—not at the Sun—and only under trusted-adult supervision.

Monday evidence checkpoint

Save after the learner identifies a light source/object/surface relationship and explains shadow formation with evidence.

Tuesday · Shadow Investigation

Changing position or distance can change a shadow

Children vary one feature at a time and use observations rather than treating every shadow change as mysterious.

One variable at a time

Change the object-to-light distance, object-to-screen distance, angle/direction, or object orientation while keeping other conditions as stable as practical. Fair comparisons are easier to interpret when fewer things change at once.

Size is relational

A shadow can become larger or smaller depending on geometry. The object itself did not necessarily grow or shrink. Avoid the shortcut “closer always makes it bigger” unless the learner names what is closer to what.

Predict → change → observe → revise

  1. Choose one safe variable.
  2. Make a prediction.
  3. Change only that variable.
  4. Record what changed in shadow size, position, or shape.
  5. Repeat or reverse the change to check the pattern.
  6. Revise the explanation if the evidence disagrees.

Shape can change

Turning an object can change the outline projected on the screen. A single shadow silhouette does not reveal every property of a three-dimensional object.

Safe equipment only

No lasers, flames/candles, high-heat bulbs, electrical disassembly, or lights aimed toward eyes. A paper model or teacher demonstration is fully valid when a physical light setup is unsuitable.

Tuesday evidence checkpoint

Save after the learner changes one condition, observes a shadow effect, and revises/explains using evidence.

Wednesday · Shadow Measurement Lab

Measure change with the same baseline and unit

Children apply Week 15 measurement fairness to shadow/model lengths.

Common start

Mark the object/base point and shadow endpoint on a paper/model surface. Compare from the same starting reference; otherwise “longer” may be an artifact of misalignment.

Equal units

Use repeated equal-size tiles, paper units, grid squares, or another consistent unit with no intentional gaps or overlaps. Report both number and unit.

Measure two conditions

  1. Record condition A and its shadow/model length.
  2. Change one safe variable.
  3. Record condition B using the same baseline and unit.
  4. Compare longer/shorter/same and, when ready, the numerical difference.
  5. Check for mixed units, gaps, overlaps, or shifted starts.

Measurement limitation

A classroom model is evidence about that setup. It does not automatically prove how every outdoor shadow behaves in every place/time.

Access

Use pre-marked endpoints, tactile strips, partner placement, large units, eye-gaze choice, or adult-assisted measuring. Visual acuity and fine-motor precision are not the science target.

Wednesday evidence checkpoint

Save after the learner measures/compares two shadow conditions fairly and names the unit and one possible measurement error.

Thursday · Day/Night & Seasonal Light Lab

Earth rotates; different places receive daylight at different times

Children use a ball/globe and fixed light model to explain day/night, then compare seasonal-light patterns without treating one local winter as universal.

Day/night model

Use a stationary safe lamp symbol/light and rotate a ball/globe. The side facing the light is modeled as day; the side turned away is modeled as night. Earth’s rotation explains the repeating day/night cycle; avoid teaching that the Sun travels around Earth each day.

Model limits

The lamp-and-ball model simplifies scale, distance, atmosphere, orbit, and many other features. Models help answer specific questions; they are not miniature copies of reality.

Compare places and seasons carefully

  1. Use fictional/local daylight cards or teacher-created data.
  2. Notice a pattern such as earlier/later sunset or longer/shorter daylight.
  3. Compare two places or seasons without assuming identical weather.
  4. Use “in this place/data set…” rather than “winter is always…”
  5. Name one limitation of the model/data.

Global season accuracy

No single-season stereotype: winter can be snowy, rainy, dry, mild, dark, bright, or absent as a familiar four-season experience depending on location. Northern and Southern Hemispheres have opposite seasonal timing, and equatorial daylight variation may be smaller.

Safety in darkness/weather

Never require children to walk outside alone, enter a dark room, navigate by flashlight, observe at night, or experience cold/snow. Use supervised classroom models, photos, drawings, or provided data.

Thursday evidence checkpoint

Save after the learner uses an Earth-rotation model for day/night and makes one globally careful seasonal-light comparison.

Friday · Light Scientist Conference

Retrieve, measure, explain, and correct misconceptions

Friday consolidates Week 17 before formal mastery; it is not a hidden test.

Retrieval round

  1. Explain how a shadow forms.
  2. Describe one variable that can change a shadow.
  3. Measure/compare a modeled shadow fairly.
  4. Use Earth rotation to explain day/night.
  5. State one reason winter/daylight patterns are not identical everywhere.

Repair the claim

Correct statements such as “a shadow comes out of an object,” “the object grew because its shadow grew,” “the Sun goes around Earth every day,” “winter means snow everywhere,” or “looking toward the Sun helps us find shadows.”

Integrated scientist task

Given a safe classroom light/shadow model or diagram, identify source/object/surface, predict a shadow, change one variable, measure/compare the result with a consistent unit, and explain what the model can and cannot show. Then use a globe/ball model to explain day/night.

Remediation

If shadow formation is unstable, return to source→object→screen diagrams. If measurement is unstable, return to Week 15 common-start/equal-unit work. If day/night reasoning slips into apparent-motion language, rotate the Earth model again.

Reflection

Ask: “What changed your explanation this week?” Accept speech, sign, AAC, tactile/model response, drawing, gesture, or dictation.

Friday evidence checkpoint

Save after retrieval, misconception repair as needed, and an integrated light/shadow/day-night explanation.

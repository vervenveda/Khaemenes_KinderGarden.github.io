Accuracy, Safety & Access Commitments

State ≠ new substance: ice and liquid water are both H₂O at different states. Water vapor is the gaseous state and is ordinarily invisible. The visible white cloud above hot water is made mainly of tiny liquid droplets after vapor cools—not “water vapor you can see.”
No hazardous temperature demonstrations: no boiling water, open flame, hot plate, dry ice, liquid nitrogen, salt/ice skin experiments, tasting investigation materials, walking on ice, or prolonged bare-skin contact with freezer-cold objects. Adults control refrigeration and any warm/cold equipment.
Access: no home freezer, snow, cold climate, thermometer reading, fine-motor pouring, standing, vision-only cloud observation, speech, or live weather exposure is required. Use sealed samples, photos, tactile-safe models, teacher data, AAC, audio description, partner support, or adult scribing.

Monday · Water State Detectives

The same water can be solid, liquid, or gas

Children distinguish state from substance and use properties rather than appearance alone.

Solid ice

Ice is solid water. It keeps its own shape until melting changes it. A particular ice piece may contain bubbles, color, minerals, or other materials; those extras do not change the central idea that frozen water is solid H₂O.

Liquid water

Liquid water flows and takes the shape of its container while keeping its amount unless water is added, removed, spilled, evaporates, freezes, or otherwise moves.

State evidence sort

  1. Use teacher-created cards showing ice, liquid water, and water-vapor situations.
  2. Name the state using provided evidence.
  3. Identify what stays the same: it is still water.
  4. Identify what changes: shape/flow/state and energy conditions.
  5. Place uncertain examples in “need more evidence” rather than guessing.

Water vapor is invisible

Water vapor is ordinarily invisible. A visible mist/fog/cloud is tiny liquid droplets and/or ice crystals suspended in air, not a view of individual gaseous water molecules.

State words are not weather labels

Solid/liquid/gas describe matter states; rainy/snowy/foggy/cloudy describe weather observations or conditions. Keep the scientific categories separate.

Monday evidence checkpoint

Save after the learner distinguishes solid/liquid/gas water, explains that state can change while the substance remains water, and corrects the visible-water-vapor misconception.

Tuesday · Melting & Freezing Fair-Test Lab

Warming and cooling can change water’s state; a fair comparison controls conditions

Children sequence state changes and compare safe teacher-provided evidence.

Melting

When solid ice gains enough thermal energy under suitable conditions, it melts into liquid water. “Heat” need not mean dangerous hot temperatures; room conditions can melt ice.

Freezing

When liquid water loses enough thermal energy under suitable conditions, it freezes into solid ice. Cold does not “enter” the water as a substance. We describe energy transfer and changing conditions.

Fair comparison

  1. Use safe equal-size model ice pieces, teacher-recorded observations, or picture sequences.
  2. Choose one comparison variable, such as location/temperature condition.
  3. Keep comparison features as similar as practical: starting amount/size, container, observation interval, and measurement method.
  4. Predict which changes sooner and why.
  5. Observe provided results and revise the prediction if needed.

Sequence the evidence

Use first/next/then/finally to arrange a melting or freezing sequence. A sequence is evidence of change over time; it is not proof of an exact temperature unless temperature was actually measured.

Reversible does not mean instant

Liquid water can freeze and ice can melt, but the rate depends on conditions. Different containers, volumes, airflow, surfaces, dissolved materials, and temperatures can affect timing.

Wednesday · Evaporation & Condensation Lab

Liquid water can become invisible vapor without boiling—and vapor can become liquid droplets

Children reason from safe observations and avoid the “disappeared forever” misconception.

Evaporation

At a liquid surface, some water molecules can enter the air as water vapor. Evaporation can happen below the boiling point. A wet surface drying does not mean the water stopped existing; much of it moved into the surrounding air as vapor.

Condensation

When water vapor encounters conditions that favor the gas changing to liquid, droplets can form. The droplets on the outside of a cold sealed cup can come from water vapor in surrounding air—not necessarily from water leaking through the cup.

Evidence-model sequence

  1. Study teacher-provided before/after drying evidence or a safe sealed model.
  2. Identify liquid water at the start.
  3. Model water moving into air as invisible vapor.
  4. Study a cold-surface condensation image/model.
  5. Model vapor changing back to liquid droplets.
  6. Name what the model does not show directly: individual molecules are too small to see.

Visible “steam” correction

The visible white cloud near hot water is not the invisible water vapor itself. It forms as vapor cools/condenses into tiny droplets. No hot-water demonstration is required.

Amount evidence has limits

If a shallow dish contains less liquid later, evaporation may be a reasonable explanation when spill/use are controlled—but one observation does not measure exactly where every water molecule went.

Save after the learner explains evaporation below boiling, condensation on a cool surface, and why visible mist is not gaseous water vapor itself.

Thursday · Weather-Water Evidence Lab

Clouds and precipitation connect water-state evidence to weather—but weather varies by conditions and place

Children connect weather observations to water while avoiding cartoon water-cycle claims.

What is a cloud?

Clouds are made of many tiny liquid water droplets and/or ice crystals suspended in the atmosphere. Water vapor itself is invisible. Cloud type/appearance alone does not tell us every condition inside the cloud.

Precipitation

Rain, snow, sleet/ice pellets, and other precipitation forms depend on atmospheric conditions. A cloudy sky does not guarantee precipitation, and cold weather does not guarantee snow.

Observe → record → compare

  1. Use safe teacher-provided weather cards/data or an appropriate current observation.
  2. Record visible cloud/precipitation information separately from temperature or forecast information.
  3. Build or read a simple multi-day category graph.
  4. Compare counts and make one evidence-supported claim.
  5. Name one thing the small data set cannot guarantee about tomorrow or another location.

Water-cycle model limits

A classroom cycle diagram can show evaporation, condensation/cloud formation, precipitation, collection/runoff, freezing/melting, and movement. But real water pathways are complex and do not follow one single circular route in a fixed order.

No climate stereotype

Do not teach “winter = snow” or “rain = warm place.” Weather and precipitation vary with location, season, altitude, atmospheric conditions, and day-to-day changes.

Thursday evidence checkpoint

Save after the learner explains cloud composition accurately, interprets a small weather-water data set, and names a forecast/model/location limitation.

Friday · Water Change Scientist Conference

Integrate state, change, evidence, weather, and safety

Friday consolidates Week 22 before formal mastery; it is not a hidden test or dangerous temperature challenge.

Retrieval round

  1. Identify solid, liquid, and gaseous water.
  2. Explain melting and freezing directions.
  3. Explain evaporation without requiring boiling.
  4. Explain condensation and the cold-cup example.
  5. Explain what clouds contain and why water vapor itself is invisible.

Repair the claims

Correct statements such as “ice is a different substance from water,” “cold goes into water,” “evaporation only happens when water boils,” “the white cloud above hot water is water vapor,” “clouds are bags of gas,” “cloudy means it will rain,” or “winter always means snow.”

Integrated water evidence task

Given a safe picture/data sequence, identify state changes, arrange events, choose a fair-comparison feature, explain an evaporation/condensation step, interpret a small weather graph, and name one model/data limitation plus one safety boundary.

Safety is part of scientific competence

No boiling, flame, dry ice, liquid nitrogen, intentional slipping/freezing exposure, tasting, or skin-chilling demonstration is needed to understand water-state change. Safer representations do not lower the academic target.

Reflection

Complete through any accessible mode: “Water can change state when…,” “One thing people often mistake for water vapor is…,” or “A fair water experiment should keep…”.

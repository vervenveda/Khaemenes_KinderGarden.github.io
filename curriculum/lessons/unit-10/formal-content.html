Maps, Places & Community

Learn how maps turn places into useful models using symbols, labels, relative position, routes, and consistent distance units—without exposing anyone's real location.

Essential Question: How can a map help us explain where things are, how places connect, and how to travel safely through a model?

Week 10 Learning Arc

Continuity: Week 7 built spatial language; Week 9 explored belonging and place. Week 10 turns those ideas into map models and route reasoning.

Safety, Privacy & Access

No real-location requirement: never require a home address, live location, school route, bus stop, identifying landmark, exact neighborhood, or geolocation. Fictional/classroom/model maps fully satisfy the learning goals.

Color is never the only map cue. Use shape, texture, icon, label, number, or tactile marker too.

Movement is optional: “distance” may be measured with equal grid moves, counters, tiles, paper units, wheelchair-accessible model moves, or another consistent unit. A child's stride length or mobility is not the academic target.

Monday · Map Model Detectives

A map is a model, not the place itself

Children compare real or imagined spaces with simplified overhead-style representations and notice that maps include selected information.

Concept

Place vs representation

A classroom, park, market, or imaginary island is a place. A map is a smaller representation that uses position, symbols, labels, and chosen details to help someone reason about that place.

Perspective

Look from above

Arrange four objects on a table. Draw or place four symbols as if looking down. Turn the paper and ask what changed: the orientation changed, but the relative relationships can remain.

Important: north-up is a useful convention on many maps, not a rule that every map must use.

Apply

Build a fictional mini-place

🌳□＋○
  1. Choose four fictional/classroom places.
  2. Arrange models or symbols.
  3. Describe two spatial relationships: beside, above/below on the page, between, near, far, left/right relative to the chosen orientation.
  4. Explain one thing the map leaves out.

Map limitation

Maps are made for purposes. No single map shows everything. A simple playground map and a transit map can show the same area differently because they answer different questions.

Family connection

Sketch an imaginary room, garden, village, spaceship, or story setting. Do not map a real home unless a family independently chooses to do so.

Monday evidence checkpoint

Save after the learner has explained that a map is a model and demonstrated at least two positional relationships.

Tuesday · Symbol & Legend Lab

Make map symbols mean something

Children create a consistent symbol system and use a legend/key so another person can interpret the map.

Symbol + key

★ = meeting place
▦ = garden
● = water station

A symbol only helps if its meaning is consistent and the reader can learn what it represents.

Labels help

Add words, initial letters, numbers, tactile marks, or AAC-supported labels. A legend should not rely only on color.

Design a three-symbol legend

  1. Choose three fictional/community places.
  2. Create three distinguishable symbols.
  3. Build a legend/key.
  4. Place symbols on a model map.
  5. Give the map to another person and see whether they can decode it.

If they misread it, revise the symbol or legend. Revision is evidence of communication thinking.

Accessible mapmaking

Large pieces, tactile textures, high contrast, labels, dictation, pointing, eye gaze, AAC, or adult-assisted placement are valid. The target is symbolic representation.

Mentor question

“If ★ means library in the key, can ★ suddenly mean pond in one corner?” Explain why consistency matters.

Tuesday evidence checkpoint

Save after the learner has created and used a consistent legend/key that another person can interpret.

Wednesday · Route & Distance Lab

Follow directions and compare routes fairly

Children sequence route directions and compare distances using one consistent model unit rather than body-dependent step size.

Route language

Practice start, stop, forward, turn, beside, between, past, before, after, near/far, and landmark language on a fictional grid.

Directions are relative to a starting position and orientation, so state the start clearly.

Consistent units

Compare Route A = 5 grid moves and Route B = 8 grid moves. With the same unit, Route A is shorter by 3 moves.

Do not compare children's physical step counts as if every step were the same length.

Route cards

  1. Choose a start and destination on a fictional map.
  2. Build a 3–6 move route with arrows, words, symbols, tactile pieces, or verbal directions.
  3. Have another person follow it.
  4. Compare it with a second route using the same unit.
  5. Revise ambiguous instructions.

Safety reasoning

Real-world travel requires trusted-adult rules, traffic awareness, accessibility needs, and local safety guidance. This lesson does not authorize a child to travel independently.

Multiple valid routes

A shortest route is not always the safest, most accessible, or most useful route. On a model, discuss why someone might choose a longer path.

Wednesday evidence checkpoint

Save after the learner has followed or created a sequenced route and compared two routes with the same model unit.

Thursday · Community Planners

Maps can help us think about community needs

Children design a fictional community map and explain why different people may need different places and routes.

Community places

Possible examples include homes, food markets, clinics, libraries, parks, schools, places of worship, transit, water, community centers, workspaces, emergency services, cultural spaces, or other locally relevant places. No fixed list defines every community.

Needs vary

Ask: “Who needs to reach this place? What route would be usable for someone with a stroller, wheelchair, visual impairment, young child, or heavy load?” Avoid presenting disability as a problem to fix.

Plan an inclusive fictional community

  1. Choose 5–7 places.
  2. Create a symbol + legend for each category.
  3. Place at least two routes.
  4. Mark one route feature that supports access or safety.
  5. Explain one tradeoff: near vs quiet, short vs accessible, direct vs safe, etc.

Civic reasoning: maps can reveal whose needs were considered and whose may have been left out.

Privacy reminder

Do not copy a learner's exact home, route, or schedule into the community map. Fictionalizing is not “less authentic”; it protects privacy while preserving the reasoning goal.

Revision

Ask another person to navigate the plan. If the map is confusing or excludes an important need, revise it and explain why.

Thursday evidence checkpoint

Save after the learner has created a fictional community map with a key, routes, and one explained accessibility/safety design choice.

Friday · Navigator Challenge

Read, route, compare, revise, explain

Friday retrieves the whole week before formal mastery.

Five quick asks

  1. Explain map vs real place.
  2. Decode a symbol using a legend.
  3. Use two positional/directional words accurately.
  4. Compare two route lengths using the same unit.
  5. Name one location detail a child never has to reveal for this course.

Map debugging

Find one problem in a sample map: missing key, inconsistent symbol, unclear start, route through an obstacle, color-only coding, or exposed private location. Explain the correction.

Navigator performance

  1. Read a fictional map with symbols and a legend.
  2. Choose a start/destination.
  3. Give or follow a route.
  4. Measure/compare it with a second route using equal units.
  5. Explain one safety/access/privacy improvement.

Speech, sign, AAC, tactile map, drawing, object placement, or home language are valid evidence forms.

Reflection

Complete: “A map helps me ____. A map does not show ____. I can protect location privacy by ____.”

Portfolio

Save a fictional map, legend, route card, teacher observation, or model photo that contains no personally identifying location data.

Friday evidence checkpoint

Save only after retrieval/remediation and the Navigator Challenge. This confirms the learning sequence is complete; it does not award mastery.

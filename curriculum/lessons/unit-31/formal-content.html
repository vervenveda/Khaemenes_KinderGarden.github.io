Monday: Tiny Worlds Observation Detectives

Daily learning target: Observe a small garden animal or original drawing without disturbing it, describe two visible body features, and distinguish something noticed from a guess.

Morning Circle: Welcome song, visual schedule, feelings check-in, and question of the day: “What tiny living things can we observe?”

Literacy: Observation words and detail labels

Math: Count legs/wings and graph garden life

Inquiry: Animal structures and habitats

SEL: Gentle observation

Original guided activity: Use educator-drawn illustrations of an ant, butterfly, and snail, or observe a living animal outdoors from a safe distance with an adult. Model the difference between “I see six legs” and “I think it is looking for food.” Invite children to choose one animal, draw its shape, and label two features such as wings, legs, antennae, or shell where applicable. Explain that a snail is not an insect and that butterflies have six legs even when some are difficult to see. Do not capture, touch, feed, or disturb wildlife; indoor picture-based participation is equally valid.

Practice evidence (not formal mastery): Learner identifies two accurate features of one pictured animal and shares one observation without presenting an unsupported guess as fact. Support with two simple picture choices and adult modeling; extend by comparing a snail and an ant using one observed similarity and difference. Speaking, pointing, drawing, or accessible communication are all accepted.

Centers: Teacher table, Math/manipulative bin, Art/maker station, Dramatic play, Movement/music, Book corner, Calm reflection spot

Teacher evidence: Adult observation, child explanation, work sample, oral language, cooperative play, and assessment item.

Tuesday: Garden Creature Counting Lab

Daily learning target: Classify six original animal picture cards by an observable feature, count each group using one-to-one correspondence, and represent the counts in a simple picture graph.

Morning Circle: Welcome song, visual schedule, feelings check-in, and question of the day: “What tiny living things can we observe?”

Literacy: Observation words and detail labels

Math: Count legs/wings and graph garden life

Inquiry: Animal structures and habitats

SEL: Gentle observation

Original guided activity: Make six original picture cards: two ants, two butterflies, and two snails. Examine visible features before sorting the pictures by animal type, then place one paper counter above each category in a three-column picture graph. Touch and count every counter once, compare the column totals, and explain why a snail belongs outside the insect group. For an additional classification, group cards by visible shells versus no shells, noting that not all animals with no shell are insects. Use illustrations rather than handling living animals.

Practice evidence (not formal mastery): Learner correctly sorts at least four of the six cards, counts a selected group accurately, and interprets one graph column through speech, pointing, drawing, or accessible communication. Support with four cards and two categories; extend by changing the sorting rule and explaining why the graph changes. Give fresh feedback without resetting prior practice.

Centers: Teacher table, Math/manipulative bin, Art/maker station, Dramatic play, Movement/music, Book corner, Calm reflection spot

Teacher evidence: Adult observation, child explanation, work sample, oral language, cooperative play, and assessment item.

Wednesday: Garden Habitat Investigators

Daily learning target: Compare two illustrated microhabitats, recognize that small animals need suitable shelter, water, and food, and support a habitat choice with an observable clue.

Morning Circle: Welcome song, visual schedule, feelings check-in, and question of the day: “What tiny living things can we observe?”

Literacy: Observation words and detail labels

Math: Count legs/wings and graph garden life

Inquiry: Animal structures and habitats

SEL: Gentle observation

Original guided activity: Draw two simple garden scenes: a shaded patch with leaf litter and damp soil, and a dry sunny patch with stones and flowers. Provide original paper picture cards of an earthworm, snail, butterfly, and ant. Ask children what each scene shows before predicting where an animal might find a suitable condition; explain that animals can use more than one part of a garden and a drawing cannot prove where an animal lives. Model a careful observation such as “The soil under the leaves looks damp” and a testable question such as “Where might a worm avoid drying out?” Let children move paper cards between habitats and justify one placement using visible clues. Do not capture, move, handle, or disturb living animals; offer a fully indoor picture-based alternative.

Practice evidence (not formal mastery): Learner describes one feature of each scene, suggests a plausible animal-habitat connection, and gives one evidence-based reason. Accept verbal explanation, drawing, pointing, or accessible communication. Support with two animal cards and a choice of picture clues; extend by identifying what additional observation would help test a prediction. Model a different example when reteaching, without restarting the lesson.

Centers: Teacher table, Math/manipulative bin, Art/maker station, Dramatic play, Movement/music, Book corner, Calm reflection spot

Teacher evidence: Adult observation, child explanation, work sample, oral language, cooperative play, and assessment item.

Thursday: My Tiny Worlds Field Guide

Daily learning target: Create an original illustrated field-guide page that names one small garden animal, labels two accurate body features, and describes a plausible habitat with an observation-based reason.

Morning Circle: Welcome song, visual schedule, feelings check-in, and question of the day: “What tiny living things can we observe?”

Literacy: Observation words and detail labels

Math: Count legs/wings and graph garden life

Inquiry: Animal structures and habitats

SEL: Gentle observation

Original guided activity: Fold a sheet of paper into a simple field-guide page with four spaces: animal name, careful drawing, feature labels, and “Where it might find what it needs.” Let children choose an ant, butterfly, snail, or earthworm from original educator-made illustrations. Model how to label visible structures accurately, such as an ant’s six legs or a snail’s shell, and how to write or dictate an evidence-based sentence: “I see damp soil and leaves; a snail may find shelter here.” Explain that drawings and predictions are not proof of an actual sighting. Invite children to compare their page with a partner’s and revise one label or explanation; no living animals are handled or collected.

Practice evidence (not formal mastery): Learner provides an identifiable drawing, two accurate labels, and a habitat idea supported by one visible clue. Accept speech, dictation, pointing, tactile or pre-drawn picture choices, and accessible communication. Support with an educator-prepared four-part template; extend by adding a second animal and explaining a habitat difference. Keep the page as an optional portfolio sample, not a formal mastery receipt.

Centers: Teacher table, Math/manipulative bin, Art/maker station, Dramatic play, Movement/music, Book corner, Calm reflection spot

Teacher evidence: Adult observation, child explanation, work sample, oral language, cooperative play, and assessment item.

Friday: Young Scientists Reflection Circle

Daily learning target: Classify an unfamiliar garden animal picture by visible features, describe a plausible habitat with one observation-based reason, and reflect on one improvement to a field-guide page.

Morning Circle: Welcome song, visual schedule, feelings check-in, and question of the day: “What tiny living things can we observe?”

Literacy: Observation words and detail labels

Math: Count legs/wings and graph garden life

Inquiry: Animal structures and habitats

SEL: Gentle observation

Original guided activity: Present two new original educator-drawn cards showing a ladybug and an earthworm, with clear body features, plus simple pictures of a leafy garden and damp soil. Children observe without touching animals, notice the ladybug’s six legs and the earthworm’s segmented body, and explain why one is an insect and the other is not. Invite each child to match an animal to a possible suitable environment, name a visible clue, and distinguish an observation from a prediction. Children then revisit Thursday’s field-guide page, choose one accurate label or habitat explanation to improve, and share a growth moment privately, through art, or in an optional group circle. Retain the existing separate weekly mastery assessment; this activity neither replaces nor records a formal score.

Practice evidence (not formal mastery): Learner identifies one accurate feature on each new card, classifies at least one animal correctly, gives a plausible habitat reason, and communicates one field-guide revision. Accept drawing, pointing, speech, dictation, or accessible communication. Support with labeled example pictures and two choices; extend by comparing how the two animals might use different microhabitats. Reteach using fresh examples without resetting earlier work.

Centers: Teacher table, Math/manipulative bin, Art/maker station, Dramatic play, Movement/music, Book corner, Calm reflection spot

Teacher evidence: Adult observation, child explanation, work sample, oral language, cooperative play, and assessment item.

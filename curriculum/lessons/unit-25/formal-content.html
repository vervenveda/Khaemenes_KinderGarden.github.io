Work, Tools & Community Systems

Study how paid and unpaid work, tools, goods, services, questions, safety rules, and connected roles help communities function—without turning jobs into costumes or ranking people by pay, prestige, uniform, gender, or family occupation.

Dignity, Privacy & Safety Commitments

People are not job costumes: do not infer occupation, competence, income, authority, gender, race, citizenship, disability, or social value from clothing, accent, body, neighborhood, vehicle, or appearance. A uniform can be evidence only when a provided source identifies its meaning.
No forced real-worker interview: use teacher-created profiles, fictional interviews, books, recordings, or consenting invited adults. Children do not need to disclose parent/guardian jobs, unemployment, income, schedules, workplace, immigration status, military/police contact, family business, or home responsibilities.
Tools require purpose and authorization: a tool may be useful in one trained context and unsafe in another. No real knives, power tools, weapons, medication, medical equipment, electrical disassembly, construction machinery, emergency equipment, chemicals, or workplace hazards are required.
Contribution is broader than paid employment: caregiving, household work, study/training, volunteering, mutual aid, community organizing, maintenance, repair, creative work, and accessible/adapted work can contribute. Income/prestige does not measure human worth.
Monday · Work & Contribution Detectives

Reason from tasks and responsibilities—not costumes or status

Children study what a person does, what need/purpose the work addresses, and what evidence supports the inference.

Task before title

Given a fictional profile, identify an observable task such as repairing a pipe, caring for a child, preparing food, designing a sign, cleaning a public space, translating information, stocking supplies, teaching, growing food, maintaining software, or coordinating transportation. A job title is useful only when the source provides it.

Paid and unpaid contribution

Paid employment is one form of work, not the only valuable contribution. Household care, caregiving, volunteering, learning/training, mutual aid, repair, maintenance, and creative/community work can also require skill, time, responsibility, and coordination.

Evidence → task → contribution

  1. Read/view a teacher-created fictional work profile.
  2. Name one task shown or stated.
  3. Identify a need/purpose the task may serve.
  4. Name the evidence that supports the claim.
  5. Identify one thing the profile does not tell us about the person's income, identity, character, or social value.

No hierarchy of dignity

A role can differ in training, responsibility, risk, pay, or authority without making one person more worthy than another. Income, prestige, uniform, title, or education level does not measure a person's human worth.

Counter-stereotype reasoning

Reject claims such as “only men repair machines,” “only women care for children,” “a person in work clothes is less educated,” or “someone without a paid job contributes nothing.” Use evidence about the actual task/person instead.

Monday evidence checkpoint

Save after the learner uses task/source evidence to explain a contribution and rejects a job-status, gender, uniform, or paid-work stereotype.

Tuesday · Tools, Tasks & Safety Lab

A tool is chosen for a purpose—and safe use depends on context, training, and authorization

Children connect tools to functions without treating dangerous real equipment as classroom props.

Tool → function → task

A tool can be physical, digital, communication-based, or adaptive: ruler, broom, wrench, tablet, screen reader, calculator, translation board, thermometer, cart, sewing tool, software, or other provided examples. Explain what the tool helps accomplish rather than who “normally” uses it.

Same tool, different contexts

A tool's appropriateness depends on task, condition, training, age, permission, PPE/safeguards, and environment. Knowing what a tool does does not authorize a child to use it.

Match → justify → safety boundary

  1. Use picture/model/tool cards only.
  2. Match a tool to a stated task.
  3. Explain the function using evidence from the card/profile.
  4. Name one safety/access condition.
  5. Identify whether the example is child-safe classroom practice, adult/trained use, or “need more information.”
  6. Revise a match if the task changes.

Adaptive tools are real tools

Wheelchairs, hearing technology, AAC, screen readers, adapted grips, lifts, canes, service-animal support systems, and other accessibility tools can help people work, learn, communicate, navigate, or care for themselves/others. Do not describe them as evidence that a person is less capable.

Dangerous props stay fictional

Do not place real medical, emergency, construction, kitchen-blade, weapon, chemical, electrical, or industrial equipment into dramatic play simply to imitate a job. A photo/card/model or adult explanation is enough.

Tuesday evidence checkpoint

Save after the learner matches tool/function/task from evidence and explains that authorization/training/safety conditions determine whether real use is appropriate.

Wednesday · Goods, Services & Work-Data Lab

Classify the provided example, count carefully, and do not turn a small sample into a claim about society

Children connect Week 16 goods/services reasoning to a provided community-work data set.

Goods and services

A good is a physical item; a service is an action/work performed for someone or a purpose. Many real organizations/workers may be involved in both—for example a repair shop can provide a repair service and supply a replacement part.

Category rule first

State the classification rule before sorting. If a card shows both a good and service, use the category specified by the prompt or mark it “both/needs clarification” rather than forcing a false binary.

Sort → tally → graph → verify → limit

  1. Sort a provided fictional set by the stated category.
  2. Count each card once.
  3. Tally category totals.
  4. Build/read an accessible picture/object/tactile/simple bar graph.
  5. Verify totals equal the sample size.
  6. Compare categories with numbers/more/fewer/same.
  7. Name one conclusion the sample cannot justify about which work is most important, common everywhere, highest paid, or best.

Data does not rank worth

A larger bar on a classroom graph means “more examples in this provided sample,” not “more valuable people.” Frequency, pay, authority, skill, risk, importance, and human dignity are different ideas.

Access

Counting/classifying may use AAC, partner placement, enlarged/tactile cards, eye gaze, adult scribing, oral response, or another appropriate representation. Fine-motor sorting, small-print reading, speech, or color-only graphs are not required.

Wednesday evidence checkpoint

Save after the learner distinguishes goods/services from a stated rule, verifies a tally/graph, and limits conclusions to the provided sample without ranking workers.

Thursday · Respectful Interview & Community-System Lab

Good questions need consent, relevance, source awareness, and privacy boundaries

Children practice interviewing through fictional/consenting sources and map how roles depend on one another.

Question words with purpose

Practice who/what/when/where/why/how questions using a fictional profile or consenting guest. Prefer questions about tasks, training, tools, teamwork, challenges, safety, and what helps the work succeed.

Consent and privacy

An interview is not permission to ask anything. A person may decline a question. Do not require salary/income, medical/disability details, immigration/citizenship status, religion, family status, home address, trauma, political beliefs, confidential workplace information, or other private data.

Ask → source → answer → follow-up

  1. Identify the source: fictional profile, book, recording, or consenting adult.
  2. Ask one relevant respectful question.
  3. Record the answer accurately through an accessible mode.
  4. Separate what the source said from the learner's inference.
  5. Ask a follow-up based on the answer.
  6. Identify one private/inappropriate question that should not be required.

Community system map

Use fictional role cards to show a service/system such as food access, a library, transit, a school, water service, health support, emergency response, building maintenance, communications, or a public event. Map at least three roles/resources and one dependency between them.

“Helper” is not a permanent identity

People can have several roles across a day/lifetime, and one role depends on others. A teacher may also be a caregiver; a mechanic may study; a student may volunteer. Communities work through interdependence, not a ranking of “important helpers.”

Thursday evidence checkpoint

Save after the learner asks/uses a respectful source-based question, separates source from inference, respects a privacy boundary, and explains at least one dependency in a fictional community system.

Friday · Work, Dignity & Interdependence Conference

Integrate task evidence, tools, goods/services, data, sources, dignity, safety, and systems

Friday consolidates Week 25 before formal mastery.

Retrieval round

  1. Explain why clothing/uniform alone cannot prove a person's job or competence.
  2. Give one paid and one unpaid contribution without ranking worth.
  3. Match a tool to a task and name an authorization/safety condition.
  4. Distinguish a good from a service using a provided example.
  5. Explain why a small work graph cannot tell which job is “best” or most valuable.

Repair the claims

Correct statements such as “only men use tools,” “only paid work counts,” “a uniform tells us everything about a person,” “knowing a tool means I may use it,” “the biggest graph bar is the most important job,” “a guest must answer every interview question,” or “one helper works alone.”

Integrated community-work scenario

Given fictional role profiles, tool cards, a goods/services set, a small graph, and a community-system map: infer a task from evidence, choose a suitable tool with a safety boundary, classify/interpret one good/service example, limit a data claim, ask one respectful question, protect one privacy boundary, and explain how two or more roles/resources depend on each other.

Worker dignity & fairness

No occupation, unemployment status, income level, disability, education level, citizenship status, uniform, or family role determines a person's human worth. Respect includes accurate language, safe boundaries, fair evidence, and recognizing interdependence—not forced praise or stereotypes.

Reflection

Complete through any accessible mode: “To understand someone's work I should look for evidence about…,” “A tool is safe to use when…,” “A respectful question should…,” or “A community system depends on…”.

Friday evidence checkpoint

Save after the learner integrates task evidence, tools/safety, goods/services, checked data, respectful sources, privacy, dignity, and community-system interdependence.

Stories Across Places, Languages & Time

Study stories as told, retold, translated, adapted, and connected to people and places. Compare character, setting, sequence, motifs, and versions while using maps and source information carefully—without turning cultures into costumes, stereotypes, or a single representative tale.

Essential Question: How can we compare stories and their sources while respecting that people, places, languages, and traditions are more complex than one story?

Source, Culture & Access Commitments

Source honesty: “traditional,” “folk,” or “old” does not mean a story has no people behind it. When the exact origin is uncertain, say so. Prefer “one recorded/told version associated with…” over claiming a single story belongs to or represents every member of a group.
No culture-as-costume: do not dress children “as a culture,” imitate accents, invent sacred ceremonies, copy restricted Indigenous/religious designs, or use skin tone, nationality, disability, language, religion, or ethnicity as comic material. One folktale never represents an entire culture, country, language, or people.
Access: stories may be heard, signed, read, picture-sequenced, audio-described, summarized, modeled, or communicated through AAC/home language. No accent imitation, fluent oral performance, personal travel history, family heritage disclosure, purchased books, or internet access is required.

Monday · Story Source Detectives

A story can have a source, teller, author, translator, adapter, and more than one version

Children learn source language before treating a story as evidence about a place or people.

Story/source vocabulary

Story: the narrative. Source: where this version came from. Teller/author: a person who tells or writes. Translator: someone who represents language in another language. Adapter/reteller: someone who changes form/details for a new telling. Kindergarten learners need not memorize every term, but should understand that versions come through people.

Origin can be uncertain

Some stories have long oral histories and many communities/versions. Do not invent an exact birthplace, date, or single owner when evidence is uncertain. “We do not know exactly” is a valid source statement.

Label the evidence

  1. Use two short teacher-created or appropriately summarized story cards.
  2. Identify what source information is actually provided.
  3. Separate what the story says from what the source note says.
  4. Name one thing we still do not know.
  5. Practice “one version” language rather than “all people tell/believe…”

Story ≠ whole culture

A tale may reflect some ideas, language, history, humor, values, or artistic choices in a particular telling. One folktale never represents an entire culture, country, language, or people.

Translation is a version too

Languages do not map word-for-word perfectly. A translated or summarized story can preserve important meaning while changing wording, rhythm, rhyme, names, or explanation. Do not treat the English wording as the only “real” form.

Monday evidence checkpoint

Save after the learner distinguishes story content from source/version information and uses careful “one version / uncertain source” language.

Tuesday · Character, Setting & Sequence Lab

Compare stories with evidence—not stereotypes

Children identify character, setting, problem/action, and sequence, then compare two texts or versions by stated features.

Character and setting

Identify who/what acts and where/when the story is set using evidence in the story. A setting does not automatically describe how real people live today.

Sequence and cause

Order beginning/middle/end or first/next/last, then identify one action/result relationship when the story supports it. Do not invent motives not present in the evidence.

Same / different / evidence

  1. Compare two provided stories or two versions.
  2. Name one similarity in character, setting, sequence, problem, solution, or motif.
  3. Name one difference.
  4. Point to or retell evidence supporting each comparison.
  5. State one conclusion the comparison does not justify about real people or places.

Math: compare the provided collection

Count a stated story feature in a small provided set—such as animal characters, journeys, repeated-three structures, helpers, or settings—then compare groups with more/fewer/same. The count describes this sample, not a culture’s importance or creativity.

No “primitive/advanced” ladder

Do not rank oral, written, signed, illustrated, sung, digital, or performed storytelling as more or less civilized. Different forms serve different communities, purposes, and access needs.

Tuesday evidence checkpoint

Save after the learner compares two stories/versions using character-setting-sequence evidence and one fair count/comparison from the provided sample.

Wednesday · Story Map & Place Lab

A map can locate a place connected to a version—but a border does not define a whole culture

Children revisit maps as selective models while connecting stories to documented or fictional place context carefully.

Place evidence

If a source note identifies a region, city, language community, route, landscape, or country, locate it on an age-appropriate map/model. If place is unknown or disputed, mark it as unknown rather than guessing.

Country ≠ one culture or language

National borders do not mean everyone inside speaks one language, shares one ancestry, practices one religion, tells one set of stories, or has one way of life. Cultures and languages can cross borders; borders and names can also change over time.

Map purpose and limitation

  1. Locate a documented or fictional story place.
  2. Use a legend/key or positional language.
  3. Describe what the map helps us see.
  4. Name what it cannot tell us about a story, teller, family, language, or community.
  5. Compare two locations by a stated map feature without turning distance into cultural difference.

Travel is not required

No passport, birthplace, citizenship, immigration history, ancestry, address, travel experience, family map, or “where are you really from?” disclosure is required. Fictional/public examples are fully valid.

Time matters

A story documented in one place/time does not freeze that place in the past. Avoid using “traditional village” imagery to imply that real people from a region do not also live modern, varied lives.

Wednesday evidence checkpoint

Save after the learner uses a map/place source carefully and explains at least one map limitation or border/culture misconception.

Thursday · Version, Motif & Evidence Studio

Stories change as people retell them; change should be described, not treated as corruption

Children compare motifs and versions, graph a small evidence set, and create a respectful original variation using teacher-created material.

Version change

Retellings can change setting, details, sequence, names, language, length, audience, or ending while preserving some recognizable structure. Ask: what stayed, what changed, and what evidence shows it?

Motif is not ownership proof

A journey, trickster, transformation, repeated number, talking animal, helper, or contest can appear in many unrelated stories. A shared motif does not prove that one culture copied another or that two stories have the same origin.

Count → graph → compare → create

  1. Use a small set of teacher-created story summaries/version cards.
  2. Choose one stated motif or structural feature.
  3. Count examples with one-to-one correspondence.
  4. Build a simple object/picture graph and compare categories.
  5. Create an original fictional variation by changing one or two features.
  6. Explain which parts were inspired by the classroom structure and which were newly invented.

Respect restricted/sacred material

Do not copy, reenact, remix, costume, or craft sacred/restricted Indigenous, religious, ceremonial, funerary, initiation, or community-protected stories/symbols simply because they appear online or in a book. Use teacher-created fictional material or clearly appropriate public educational examples for remixing.

Art/music without imitation

Children may explore general story mood, pattern, rhythm, color, shape, or composition without pretending to reproduce a group’s sacred art, accent, instruments, ceremony, or “traditional sound.”

Thursday evidence checkpoint

Save after the learner compares versions/motifs, builds or interprets a simple story-evidence graph, and creates a respectful original variation without restricted copying.

Friday · Respectful Story Scholar Conference

Integrate story evidence, source honesty, mathematics, maps, and respect

Friday consolidates Week 20 before formal mastery; it does not ask a child to perform another culture.

Retrieval round

  1. Explain the difference between a story and source/version note.
  2. Compare character, setting, or sequence in two examples using evidence.
  3. Interpret a small count/graph from the provided story set.
  4. Use a map while naming one limitation.
  5. One story cannot represent a whole people/country/language. Explain why.

Repair the claims

Correct statements such as “this story shows how everyone in that country lives,” “a border means one culture,” “traditional means old-fashioned people,” “English is the real version,” “the same motif proves the same origin,” or “if it is online, it is okay to copy a sacred story/design.”

Integrated story scholar task

Given two age-appropriate provided story/version cards plus source notes and a map, identify source information, compare story evidence, locate/place context carefully, count or graph one stated feature, name an uncertainty or model limitation, and recommend a respectful way to retell or discuss the material.

Identity boundary

No learner is required to speak for a culture, religion, nationality, race, language, disability group, immigrant experience, or family tradition. Personal knowledge may be shared voluntarily but is never treated as the class’s authoritative source.

Reflection

Complete through any accessible mode: “A respectful story researcher checks…,” “One thing a map cannot tell me is…,” or “One way two versions can be different without one being wrong is…”.

Friday evidence checkpoint

Save after the learner integrates source/version reasoning, story evidence, map limits, mathematical comparison, and a cultural-respect boundary.

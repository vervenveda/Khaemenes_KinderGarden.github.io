Animals & Habitats

Use evidence to describe animal structures, behaviors, needs, groups, and habitats. Build a habitat model that explains how resources and conditions support life—without touching, feeding, chasing, or capturing wildlife.

Essential Question: How do an animal's structures, behaviors, and habitat help it meet its needs?

Week 12 Learning Arc

Monday: distinguish observations about animal structures and behaviors from guesses.

Tuesday: classify animals by stated evidence-based rules and count/compare groups.

Continuity: Week 11 compared plant structures and needs; Week 12 asks how animal structures, behaviors, and habitats help meet needs.

Universal Access & Wildlife Safety

No live animal, zoo visit, pet, wildlife encounter, purchased diorama kit, or internet video is required. Use locally appropriate photos, drawings, models, stories, teacher-created cards, or familiar animals.

Humane boundary: Wild animals are observe-from-a-distance. Do not feed, touch, chase, capture, corner, handle, remove nests/eggs, or approach an injured or unknown animal. A trusted adult handles wildlife concerns.
Access: AAC, sign, gesture, tactile animal shapes, high-contrast cards, eye gaze, dictation, home-language explanation, seated movement, and adult-assisted counting are valid. Animal sound imitation is optional, never required.

Monday · Animal Evidence Detectives

Notice structure, behavior, and evidence

Children describe what can actually be observed, then use careful language for possible functions.

Structure clues

beak / mouth
feet / fins
fur / feathers / scales
eyes / ears / whiskers

Ask what each structure may help an animal do. Avoid claiming every structure has only one job.

Behavior is not a personality label

“The bird pecked the ground” is an observation. “The bird is greedy” is a human-like judgment. Practice evidence-first language.

Observation or guess?

  1. Study two animal images/models.
  2. Name one visible structure and one observable behavior or pose.
  3. Say: “I notice…” before “I think this may help…”
  4. Use first sounds/alliteration with animal names if appropriate; animal sounds themselves are not universal labels and imitation is optional.

Movement

Represent crawl, hop, swim, fly, slither, or walk with a toy, gesture, AAC choice, finger path, or seated movement. Physical imitation is not required.

Wildlife respect

Discuss why distance protects both people and animals. Looking is learning; touching is not required.

Monday evidence checkpoint

Save after the learner gives an evidence-based animal observation and distinguishes it from a guess or human-like story.

Tuesday · Classify & Count Lab

Sort by a rule you can explain

Children classify the same animals in more than one valid way, then count and compare groups.

State the rule first

Classification rule must be stated before sorting. Examples: has feathers / does not; lives mostly in water / on land; two legs / four legs / other; moves mainly by flying / swimming / walking.

One animal can fit different systems

A duck can be grouped by feathers, movement, habitat, number of legs, or other evidence. Classification depends on the question and rule.

Sort, count, compare

  1. Sort 8–12 cards with one rule.
  2. Count each group with one-to-one correspondence.
  3. Compare using more, fewer, same, difference, or numbers.
  4. Re-sort the same animals using a different rule and explain what changed.

Accuracy boundary

Do not teach “all birds fly,” “all fish are tiny,” or other absolute shortcuts. Use some/not-all language and counterexamples.

Literacy

Build alliteration or beginning-sound chains with animal words in English or another familiar language when appropriate. Do not force English pronunciation as the only valid language pathway.

Tuesday evidence checkpoint

Save after the learner states a classification rule, sorts accordingly, counts groups, and compares them.

Wednesday · Habitat Fit Lab

A habitat provides resources and conditions

Children connect food, water, shelter, space, air/oxygen, temperature, and other conditions to particular animals.

Habitat is more than scenery

A habitat is not a decorative background. It includes living and nonliving features that can provide resources and conditions an organism uses.

Match with evidence

Compare a frog, camel, penguin, squirrel, fish, or locally relevant animals. Ask which habitat features fit each animal and why—not simply which picture “looks right.”

Need → feature → evidence

  1. Choose an animal.
  2. Name a need: water, food, shelter, oxygen/air, suitable temperature, space, nesting/resting place, etc.
  3. Choose a habitat feature that can help meet that need.
  4. Explain the match using a structure, behavior, or known natural-history clue.

Change and limits

Habitat conditions change. An animal may move, adjust behavior, use a different resource, or fail to thrive. One animal example does not prove a rule for every species.

Care vs rescue

Kindness to wildlife often means leaving it undisturbed and telling a trusted adult, not taking it home.

Wednesday evidence checkpoint

Save after the learner connects at least two animal needs to habitat resources/conditions with a reasonable explanation.

Thursday · Habitat Model Engineers

Build a model that explains, then revise it

Children design a fictional/model habitat around an animal's needs instead of making a decorative craft.

Model requirements

Include an animal/model plus at least four labeled or explained features: food/resource, water or moisture where relevant, shelter/resting place, space/path, oxygen/air/water condition, temperature/shade, nesting feature, or another species-appropriate need.

Model limits

A model cannot include everything. State one thing the model leaves out—seasonal change, predators, disease, migration, social groups, exact scale, or another factor.

Test the habitat

  1. Trace how the animal reaches each resource.
  2. Ask whether a feature conflicts with another need.
  3. Have a partner/adult ask one evidence question.
  4. Revise one part and explain why the revision makes the model clearer or more suitable.

No purchase requirement

Paper, recycled packaging, blocks, loose parts, drawing, tactile symbols, or a digital-free oral plan all count. A shoebox diorama is optional, not the goal.

Humane design

Do not use live animals in the model. Do not collect nests, feathers, eggs, shells occupied by animals, or other wildlife material that should remain in place.

Thursday evidence checkpoint

Save after the learner builds/explains a habitat model with need-linked features and makes or explains one evidence-based revision.

Friday · Animal Scientist Conference

Retrieve, explain, correct, and prepare for mastery

Friday is retrieval and remediation first; the formal mastery rubric opens only after all daily evidence is complete.

Five retrieval prompts

  1. Name one animal structure and possible function.
  2. State a classification rule and place an animal using evidence.
  3. Count/compare two animal groups.
  4. Connect a habitat feature to an animal need.
  5. State one humane wildlife rule.

Misconception clinic

Correct statements such as “all birds fly,” “habitat just means the background,” “kindness means feeding every wild animal,” or “an animal belongs in one group only.”

Scientist explanation

Use speech, sign, AAC, drawing, model, tactile pieces, gesture, or home language to explain: “This animal can meet this need because…” Include structure/behavior + habitat evidence.

Reflection

Name one idea that became more precise this week and one question still worth investigating.

Mastery boundary

Finishing Friday does not itself award mastery. Formal Week 12 mastery still requires at least 80% overall plus every required core criterion.

Friday evidence checkpoint

Save after retrieval, misconception repair, and an integrated animal-habitat explanation.
